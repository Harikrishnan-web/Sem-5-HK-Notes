# WEEK 10 — Community-Based Participatory Research in Context to Health Promotion

## Lecture 1 — Lecture #46: Community-Based Participatory Research in Context to Health Promotion (Part I)



### **Community-Based Participatory Action Research (CBPAR)**

* **CBPAR** is a collaborative research approach in which **all stakeholders are involved throughout the research process**.
* Community participation can extend from:

  * Formulating the research question
  * Developing data-collection tools
  * Collecting data
  * Analysing data
  * Disseminating findings
* CBPAR is a **research framework designed to address practical concerns of people in a community**.
* It begins with:

  * A community issue
  * A proposed action
  * A proposed strategy
* Research is then used to **support or enhance that community action**.

### **Three Core Characteristics of CBPAR**

* **Community-based**

  * Research is grounded in the:

    * Needs
    * Issues
    * Concerns
    * Strategies
      of communities.

* **Participatory**

  * Communities are directly engaged in:

    * The research process
    * Generation and use of community knowledge
    * Research outcomes

* **Action-based and action-oriented**

  * Research supports or enhances strategic action.
  * The ultimate aim is **community transformation and social change**. 

### **Participatory Action**

* The main purpose is to enable communities to:

  * Understand their own situation
  * Analyse their own situation
  * Interpret collected data
  * Convert their learning into action
* Rather than outsiders doing all the analysis, the **community itself participates in understanding and interpreting findings**.
* **Community knowledge is irreplaceable**.

  * It provides important insights.
  * It can help **ground-truth administrative data**.
* Complex social issues may not be fully understood or resolved through **expert research alone**.
* Interventions designed externally have sometimes produced disappointing results.
* Communities should have **equal inclusion and collaboration** in:

  * Identifying community issues
  * Researching the issues
  * Resolving the issues
* Knowledge held by:

  * Individuals
  * Families
  * Other community members
    has value and legitimacy. 

### **Key Idea**

* CBPAR connects **research + community participation + action**.
* The research process is not simply about collecting information.
* It aims to help the community **understand its situation and use that knowledge to create action and social change**.
* At every stage, there can be different **degrees of community participation**.
* Well-defined steps guide:

  * Development
  * Implementation
  * Monitoring
  * Implementation of a community action plan
    through participatory processes.

# Lecture 2 — Lecture #47: Community-Based Participatory Research in Context to Health Promotion (Part II)



### **Steps of CBPAR**

The lecture describes the major stages involved in conducting CBPAR.

### **Step 1 — Design and Implementation**

**Objective: Identify the research question and geographic focus.**

* Begin with:

  * Community needs
  * Community strengths
  * Community interests
  * Community capacity to take action
* Identify:

  * What issue needs to be addressed?
  * What are the research questions?
  * What do we want to know?
  * What hypothesis do we want to test?
  * What needs to be highlighted or brought to attention?
* Consider whether the issue:

  * Is already known
  * Is not sufficiently demonstrated
  * Can realistically be acted upon
* Define the **rough geographic boundaries** of the community to be engaged.
* The researchers' definition of the community may differ from the community's own understanding.
* Therefore, sufficient time should be allowed to:

  * Discuss community boundaries
  * Adjust the boundaries where necessary. 

### **Step 2 — Partner / Community Engagement**

**Objective: Identify action-research partners and invite them to collaborate.**

* Identify key stakeholders based on:

  * The issue
  * Geographic area
* Determine:

  * Who should be collaborated with?
  * Whether the selected group represents different constituents of the community
  * What level of participation is expected from each participant/group
* Clarify each participant's expectations.
* Determine:

  * Their interests
  * Why they want to participate
  * How they want to contribute
  * What resources they have available. 

### **Step 3 — Data Collection**

**Objective: Refine research questions, select and implement data-collection methods, and identify key participants.**

* Reconsider the research questions after understanding:

  * The issue
  * The participants
* Determine:

  * Who data should be collected from
  * Who has specific knowledge of the topic
  * How many participants are needed
  * What sample would be representative
* Consider:

  * How findings will be used
  * Who the target audience for the findings will be
* Identify the specific data required to answer the research question.
* Data may combine:

  * Community knowledge
  * Administrative data
* Example:

  * Community perception of safety can be compared with crime statistics.
* Determine:

  * What information is already available
  * What information needs to be collected
  * Whether collected data will both answer the research question **and provide a basis for action**. 

### **Data-Collection Tools and Techniques**

Participatory research can use different tools according to the type of information required.

* **Space-related tools**

  * Social map
  * Resource map
  * Mobility map
  * Transect map

* **Time-related tools**

  * Timeline
  * Time trends
  * Seasonal diagrams
  * Historical maps

* **Relation-related tools**

  * Venn diagram
  * Pair ranking
  * Matrix ranking. 

### **Transect Maps**

* Transect maps are maps of the main **areas/zones of a community**.
* They are developed through:

  * Systematic walking
  * Direct observation
* They compare:

  * Features
  * Resources
  * Uses
  * Problems
    across different zones.
* A transect can be constructed by:

  * Walking through an area with community key informants
  * Directly observing features and factors
  * Recording observations
  * Talking to people encountered during the walk.
* Issues that may be studied include:

  * Water and sanitation
  * Food availability and access
  * Occupational hazards and risks. 

### **Conducting a Transect Walk**

**Step 1 — Preparation**

* Select a route that passes through the chosen area.
* The route should cover as many:

  * Physical areas
  * Social categories of people
    as possible.
* If the geographic area is large:

  * Multiple routes can be selected.
* Select a group of community members to participate.

**Step 2 — Introduce the Purpose**

* Explain the purpose of the walk.
* Focus observations and conversations on a specific community problem.
* Example:

  * Water and sanitation
* Use opportunities to gather rich and varied information flexibly.
* Route and timing can be varied:

  * Day vs. night
  * Different routes
* This helps capture different contextual information.
* Camera/video recorder may be used when appropriate.
* Participants should record relevant things they:

  * See
  * Hear

**Step 3 — Conduct the Walk**

* The selected community members/key informants walk along the route.
* Stop at intervals to record observations.
* Some members should:

  * Record important information
  * Draw maps
  * Sketch important features/resources. 

### **Questions During a Transect Walk**

For water and sanitation, participants may ask:

* Is human feces visible:

  * Beside roads?
  * In fields?
  * Near water bodies?
* What relevant resources are available?

  * Household sanitary latrines
  * School latrines
  * Anganwadi-centre facilities
  * Community toilets
  * Tube wells
* What is the condition of these resources?

  * Are latrines functioning?
  * Are they clean?
  * Are tube wells functioning?
* Are any resources available but **not being used**?
* Talk to people encountered and ask:

  * What activities have been undertaken by the **Swachh Bharat Mission**?
  * What activities have been undertaken by other organisations, individuals or groups?
  * What problems exist in making the village open-defecation free?
  * What problems exist in providing safe water? 

### **Step 4 — Debriefing and Learning**

* After the walk, participants sit together and compile:

  * A diagram
  * A table
    of the main observations.
* Every participant should be encouraged to contribute.
* Discussion should explore:

  * What new things were discovered?
  * Did anything surprise the participants?
  * Which resources are most used?
  * Which are least used?
  * Why?
  * What patterns are visible?
  * What opportunities were identified?
  * How can these opportunities support community-health programmes?
* Transect diagrams can be combined with:

  * Other community maps
  * Seasonal calendars
  * Historical timelines
* This allows:

  * Triangulation
  * Identification of patterns
  * Understanding changes over time. 

### **Food Markets as a Healthy Setting**

* Health status of a setting depends on:

  * Environmental conditions
  * Risk factors.
* Food markets are important because:

  * Street-vended foods provide ready-to-eat food.
  * These foods are accessible and affordable even to lower-income community members.
* Therefore, food markets are important settings for maintaining:

  * Health
  * Nutritional status
    of urban populations.
* Disease outbreaks can be transmitted through:

  * Food
  * Live animals
    in markets.
* Therefore, markets involve:

  * Food-safety concerns
  * Occupational-health concerns.
* Prevention and control of:

  * Foodborne diseases
  * Zoonoses
    have become important public-health concerns. 

### **Vendor Health**

* Food vendors may:

  * Have a disease transmissible through food
  * Have symptoms of foodborne disease.
* If a vendor has had:

  * Cholera
  * Hepatitis A
  * Typhoid fever
  * Diarrhoea within the previous 24 hours
  * They should **not handle ready-to-eat food**.
* Proper food handling throughout the food supply chain helps ensure food safety.
* Problems requiring hygiene training include:

  * Smoking while handling food
  * Blowing the nose while handling food
  * Uncovered cuts/sores
  * Touching food with unclean utensils/tools
  * Coughing or sneezing over food
  * Handling ready-to-eat food while ill
  * Improper waste disposal. 

### **Market Sanitation Checklist**

* Adequate and appropriately placed toilets for:

  * Men
  * Women
* Hand-washing facilities
* Adequate water supply for:

  * Cleaning
  * Food preparation
* Potable drinking water
* Appropriate:

  * Walls
  * Floors
  * Drainage
* Solid waste:

  * Stored in closed containers
  * Removed regularly
  * Frequency of removal should be monitored
* Liquid waste should be appropriately disposed of.
* Market areas and food should be protected from:

  * Insects
  * Rodents
  * Other animals.
* Hygiene gradient considers:

  * Cooked/ready-to-eat foods
  * Dry foods
  * Dairy products
  * Fruits and vegetables
  * Raw meat
  * Raw fish
  * Raw poultry
  * Live animals
  * Food waste
* Raw meat, fish and poultry should be:

  * Stored on ice
  * Protected from sunlight and heat.
* Live animals and raw/cooked foods should be separated to prevent **cross-contamination**. 

### **Additional Food-Safety Practices**

* Separate equipment/utensils should be used for:

  * Raw foods
  * Cooked foods
* Vendors should wash hands with soap after using the toilet.
* Ready-to-eat foods should be cooked thoroughly, especially:

  * Meat
  * Poultry
  * Eggs
* Cooked food should not remain at room temperature for more than **2 hours**.
* Cooked food should:

  * Remain steaming hot at **more than 60°C**
  * Or be thoroughly reheated before serving.
* Safe water should be used for food preparation.
* Use:

  * Undamaged fresh foods
  * Foods processed for safety, e.g. pasteurised milk
* Fruits and vegetables, especially when eaten raw, should be washed.
* Perishable food should not be used or sold after its expiry date.

# Lecture 3 — Lecture #48: Community-Based Participatory Research in Context to Health Promotion (Part III)



## **Mobility Maps**

### **Purpose of Mobility Maps**

Mobility maps are used to:

* Understand the mobility patterns of local people:

  * Where do people go?
  * Why do they go there?
* Increase:

  * Gender sensitivity
  * Awareness
    by highlighting differences between men's and women's mobility.
* Evaluate interventions, such as:

  * Vector-borne disease-control programmes
    by examining their relationship with mobility patterns.
* Assist in:

  * Planning interventions
  * Planning projects. 

### **Steps for Mobility Mapping**

* **Step 1:** Define the person, group or community whose mobility patterns are to be studied.
* **Step 2:** Explain the purpose and begin discussion about places people visit.

  * Prepare a list of places.
* **Step 3:** Ask community members to write place names on small pieces of paper.

  * Use bold letters.
  * Symbols can be used, especially when participants are illiterate.
* **Step 4:** Draw a central circle representing the:

  * Village
  * Locality
  * Community
* **Step 5:** Place the identified locations around the central circle.

  * Connect locations to the central circle with lines.
  * Thicker lines can represent more frequent visits.
* **Step 6:** Represent additional information using symbols or writing:

  * Purpose of visit
  * Distance
  * Frequency
  * Whether the person went alone or with someone.
* **Step 7:** Ask participants whether they want to change anything on the map.
* **Step 8:** Ask participants to explain:

  * Different aspects of the map
  * What they understand from it.
* **Step 9:** Facilitator listens carefully and records necessary notes.
* **Step 10:** Copy the completed map onto paper.
* **Step 11:** Triangulate the map and details with other people in the locality. 

### **Example — Saltarpalli Village, Orissa**

Villagers created a mobility map showing:

* 15 places they visit
* Frequency of visits
* Distance from the village
* Mode of transport:

  * Bus
  * Walking
  * With or without men
* Purpose:

  * Healthcare
  * Post office
  * Market
  * etc.
* Accessibility of each place
* Participants also discussed **gender differences in mobility patterns**. 

## **Flow Diagrams**

* A **flow diagram** is a visual tool used to track:

  * Flow of resources
  * Flow of benefits
  * Negative effects
    of a programme.
* It helps explore programme impacts.
* It can represent:

  * People
  * Institutions
  * Resources
* Arrows show:

  * Flow
  * Linkages
    between different elements.
* Example:

  * Flow diagram examining the decision to spray larvicidal oils on water bodies. 

## **Venn / Chapati Diagrams**

* Used to show:

  * Important institutions
  * Important individuals
  * Relationships between them
  * Their importance in decision-making
  * Their influence over different people/groups
* **Circle size** represents relative importance/influence:

  * Larger circle → greater perceived importance.
* **Distance between circles** represents closeness of relationships:

  * Closer → closer relationship
  * Further apart → weaker relationship
* Useful when a simple participatory visual method is required to study several items, such as:

  * Institutions
  * Individuals
  * Diseases
  * Social groups
* Usually studied in relation to a small number of variables, preferably:

  * Importance
  * Perceived proximity. 

### **Example of a Chapati Diagram**

* Participants identify institutions and individuals.
* Circles of different sizes represent perceived importance.
* Larger circles = more important.
* Double lines represent closeness of relationships.
* Longer lines represent lower accessibility.
* Two-sided arrows represent **mutual relationships**. 

## **Fishbone Diagram — Cause-and-Effect Diagram**

* A fishbone diagram helps **visualize the root causes of a problem**.

### **Step 1 — Problem Statement**

* Develop a clear problem statement with consensus of the team.
* Write the problem on the right side of the paper:

  * At the head of the fish.
* Draw a line with an arrow toward the head:

  * This forms the **backbone**.

### **Step 2 — Categorization**

* Identify major categories through brainstorming.
* Connect categories to the backbone as **ribs**.
* There is no fixed number of categories.
* Example for low rates of **No Scalpel Vasectomy**:

  * Manpower
  * Procedure
  * Personal
  * Social
  * Environmental.

### **Step 3 — Contributing Factors**

* Brainstorm possible causes.
* Attach each cause to the appropriate category/rib.
* A cause may fit into more than one category.
* If so, it can be placed in multiple locations.
* This allows the team to examine whether considering the same factor from multiple perspectives changes understanding. 

### **Step 4 — Checking Identified Factors**

* Repeatedly ask **“Why?”** about identified factors.
* Example:

  * Lack of manpower
  * Why?
  * Untrained supporting staff
  * Why are staff not trained?
  * Training held irregularly/infrequently
  * Why?
  * No funding
  * Why?
  * Applications not sent in time
  * Why?
  * Staff unaware of where to apply
* This process is called the **Five Whys**.
* Five is considered a minimum manageable number of questions for reaching a suitable root cause.

### **Step 5 — Deeper Causes**

* There may be multiple branches from each smaller rib.
* These are treated as **mini-ribs**.
* Continue asking why to uncover deeper causes.

### **Step 6 — Root Causes**

* Root causes are identified by looking for causes that:

  * Repeatedly occur within categories
  * Occur across major categories. 

## **Problem Tree**

* A **problem tree** is a participatory tool for mapping:

  * Main problems
  * Causes
  * Effects
* It has three main parts:

  * **Trunk → Main problem**
  * **Roots → Causes**
  * **Branches → Effects**. 

### **Example — Child Malnutrition**

* A group of mothers agrees that malnutrition is a major health problem.
* The main problem is written:

  * **“Children Are Malnourished.”**
* If participants are not literate:

  * Pictures representing malnutrition can be used.
* Participants are told:

  * Problem = tree
  * Causes = roots
* The group discusses:

  * Why children do not have enough to eat.
* Responses are represented as roots.
* Each identified cause is then examined:

  * “Why is this happening?”
* Additional responses become deeper roots.
* Participants need enough time to:

  * Discuss problems
  * Use the diagram to remember what has already been discovered.
* Finally, participants suggest possible solutions.
* Solutions can be written:

  * In words
  * Symbolically
* These solutions are presented as **possibilities for action**, not necessarily final decisions.

# Lecture 4 — Lecture #49: Community-Based Participatory Research in Context to Health Promotion (Part IV)



## **Timelines**

* Timelines show **changes over time**.
* They are particularly useful for:

  * Monitoring
  * Evaluation
  * Understanding how things changed
  * Understanding reasons and contributing factors.
* A timeline is an illustration of key events in the life of:

  * An individual
  * Household
  * Community
  * Organisation
    over a specified period.
* Usually involves:

  * Drawing a linear timeline
  * Asking people to mark important events.
* Timelines can show the progress of a:

  * Project
  * Programme
* They can highlight:

  * Achievements
  * Challenges. 

## **Ranking and Scoring**

* Useful for comparing:

  * Preferences
  * Priorities
  * Opinions
    of different community groups.
* Individuals or groups vote on items from:

  * Most important
  * To least important
* Ranking can be combined with other methods to explore:

  * Why one problem is considered larger than another
  * Why one option is preferred over another
  * Potential solutions to priority problems. 

## **Preference / Problem Ranking**

* Considered an **easy method to use**.
* People vote to select priorities.
* Example process:

  * Participants list the main problems confronting their community regarding an issue.
  * They rank the problems according to importance.
* Rankings can also compare priorities between different groups:

  * Men vs women
  * Young vs old
  * Rich vs poor. 

### **Example — Sanitation Problems Ranked by Women**

* Problems included:

  * Lack of sufficient toilets/sanitary latrines → **Score 12**
  * Difficulty in keeping toilets clean → **Score 9**
  * Lack of piped water inside toilets → **Score 6**
  * Security of women who defecate in the open → **Score 11**
  * Construction of latrines inside houses where religious idols are kept → **Score 4**
* Scores can be represented using:

  * Seeds
  * Stones
    or similar objects. 

## **Pairwise Ranking**

### **Steps**

* **Step 1:** Identify a set of priority problems/preferences.

  * Example: measures to prevent the spread of COVID-19.
* **Step 2:** Identify possible solutions.

  * Put each solution on a separate card.
  * Use pictures/symbols instead of text where possible.
* **Step 3:** Present two cards at a time.

  * Ask the community member to choose the preferred option.
  * Ask for the reason for the choice.
  * Record the response in the appropriate cell of the priority-ranking matrix.
* **Step 4:** Repeat for the other combinations.

  * Record the criteria/reasons each time.
* **Step 5:** Present another pair.

  * Continue until all possible combinations have been considered.
* **Step 6:** Arrange problems/preferences according to the results.
* **Step 7:** Repeat the exercise with several individuals.

  * Tabulate their responses. 

### **COVID-19 Example**

Measures compared:

* Vaccination
* Proper use of masks
* Physical distancing

The pairwise comparison produced:

* Vaccination → Rank I
* Proper use of masks → Rank II
* Physical distancing → Rank III

Thus, in the example, **vaccination was the most preferred measure** for preventing spread of COVID-19. 

## **Direct Matrix Ranking**

* Direct matrix ranking uses **different criteria for decision-making**.
* Different options are scored against each criterion.
* It helps understand reasons behind local preferences.
* Example:

  * Types of complementary food preferred by mothers for infants at 6 months.

### **Example Criteria**

Options:

* Biscuits soaked in animal milk
* Khichri made with only rice and dal
* Suji and animal milk

Criteria:

* Easy to prepare
* Can be mashed
* Easy to swallow
* Readily available
* Cheap

Scores:

* Biscuits soaked in animal milk → **17**
* Khichri → **11**
* Suji + animal milk → **13**

Ranking is based on the total score.

* Highest score → first rank.
* In the example:

  * Biscuits soaked in animal milk received the highest score and ranked first. 

# **Participatory Data Analysis**

### **Step 4 — Data Analysis**

**Objective: Plan and implement analysis.**

* Develop data-collection tools based on the research question.
* Determine the most appropriate participation format.
* Develop an analysis plan.
* Decide the format for analysis:

  * Example: maps
* Ask:

  * What basic information will the data provide?
  * What should be learned about the specific issue?
  * What clear patterns are being sought?
  * How will the data/analysis address the research question?
  * What additional information needs to be collected? 

### **Ways of Analysing Participatory Data**

Analysis can involve:

* Interpreting:

  * Descriptions
  * Stories
  * Statements
  * Pictures
  * Maps
  * Diagrams
  * Other visual data
* Identifying:

  * Themes
  * Patterns
* Assessing how frequently particular:

  * Ideas
  * Themes
    are mentioned.
* Assessing the **strength of feeling** about particular issues.
* Identifying:

  * Convergence
  * Divergence
    between data from:
  * Different sources
  * Different methods
  * Different perspectives
* Identifying data gaps where:

  * More information is required
  * Existing data needs further probing.

### **Important Principle in PLA**

* Only information that is **absolutely necessary** should be collected.
* Analysis should reach only the **level of accuracy needed to inform community decision-making and action**.
* Communities should not be burdened with:

  * Huge quantities/types of data collection
  * Excessively rigorous data analysis
* The emphasis should be on understanding:

  * The community's interpretation of visual data
  * Points of convergence
  * Points of divergence
    across different sources, methods and perspectives.

### **Lecture Takeaway**

  * What format to use

# Lecture 5 — Lecture #50: Community-Based Participatory Research in Context to Health Promotion (Part V)



## **CBPAR — Iterative Cycle**

* CBPAR follows an **iterative cycle** involving:

  * Research
  * Analysis
  * Action
  * Reflection
  * Evaluation
* The cycle is repeated as needed rather than treating research as a one-time activity. 

## **Traditional Research vs Participatory Action Research**

* The lecture highlights that there are **differences in paradigms** between:

  * Traditional research
  * Participatory action research
* Participatory approaches place greater emphasis on:

  * Community involvement
  * Community knowledge
  * Action
  * Reflection
  * Collaboration
* CBPAR therefore connects the generation of knowledge with **community action and change**.

## **Challenges of CBPAR**

* Involving the community can be difficult.
* Challenges include:

  * **Divergence of perspectives**

    * Different community members/stakeholders may have different views.
  * **Power imbalances**

    * Different participants may not have equal influence.
* These challenges need to be considered when conducting participatory research. 

## **Applications of CBPAR**

CBPAR is especially common in **health promotion research**.

It has been applied to:

### **Diabetes**

* Used for:

  * Prevention
  * Management
  * Awareness-building
* Example cited:

  * Community-based participatory research to design a **faith-enhanced diabetes prevention programme**.

### **HIV/AIDS**

* Used in:

  * Prevention
  * Care
  * Treatment
* CBPR has been used as a community-based approach to HIV/AIDS-related health research.

### **Cancer**

* Used in:

  * Cancer research
  * Public-health practice
  * Prevention/awareness activities.

### **HPV Vaccination**

* CBPR has been applied to research concerning:

  * Acceptability of HPV self-sampling
  * Haitian immigrant women.

### **Ethnic Minorities and Marginalized Groups**

* CBPR is also used with:

  * Ethnic minority populations
  * Marginalized groups
  * Autistic individuals
* Participatory research can provide benefits by involving people directly in research relevant to their lives. 

### **Why CBPAR Is Used in These Communities**

CBPAR is particularly valuable because of its strength in building:

* **Trust**
* **Acceptance**
* **Community engagement**

This makes participatory approaches particularly relevant when research involves communities that may be underserved, marginalized, or difficult to engage through conventional research approaches.

### **What Each Tool Does**

* **Transect map** → observes different community zones through systematic walking.
* **Mobility map** → shows where people go, why, how often and mobility differences, including gender.
* **Flow diagram** → shows movement/flow of resources, benefits or effects.
* **Venn/Chapati diagram** → shows institutions/individuals, importance, relationships and accessibility.
* **Fishbone diagram** → identifies causes and root causes.
* **Problem tree** → maps problem, causes and effects.
* **Timeline** → shows important events/changes over time.
* **Problem ranking** → prioritizes problems.
* **Pairwise ranking** → compares two options at a time.
* **Direct matrix ranking** → scores options against multiple criteria.

### **Data Analysis**

* Interpret visual and descriptive information.
* Identify themes and patterns.
* Examine frequency of ideas/themes.
* Assess strength of feelings.
* Find convergence/divergence.
* Identify gaps requiring further information.
* Collect and analyse **only the information necessary for community decision-making/action**.

### **Reporting**

* **What?** → What is the story/research question?
* **Who?** → Who is the audience?
* **When?** → Ongoing problem or particular point in time?
* **How?** → Report, website, community meeting, etc.
* Engage:

  * Community
  * CBOs
  * Elected officials
  * Other stakeholders
* Ground-truth findings with residents.
* Disseminate findings for:

  * Awareness
  * Advocacy
  * Action
