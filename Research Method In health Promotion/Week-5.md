# Qualitative Research in Health Promotion

* Qualitative research is required in health promotion for several important reasons.
* It helps in **understanding the diverse causes or determinants of a problem**.

  * Determinants may be:

    * Biological
    * Behavioral
    * Social
    * Environmental
* It helps in **developing solutions or interventions** that can:

  * Prevent a health problem
  * Mitigate an existing health problem
* It facilitates **better implementation or delivery of solutions** through:

  * Evidence-based policies
  * Evidence-based programs
* It helps in **evaluating the impact of solutions** on:

  * The level of a problem
  * The distribution of a problem
* Research in health promotion follows the same broad research steps as other types of research. 

# What is Qualitative Research?

* Qualitative research is a **field of inquiry characterized by naturalistic and interpretive practices**.
* **Naturalistic**

  * Qualitative approaches take place in the natural environment.
  * The setting is not one in which strict control is the main factor in the research design.
  * Researchers study people and phenomena in their real-world context.
* **Interpretive**

  * It involves constructing meaning from collected data.
  * Researchers interpret the information to explain the phenomenon being studied.
* Qualitative research uses **contextual and in-depth procedures**.

  * It provides a rich exploration of:

    * Complex social experiences
    * Complex social phenomena 

# Basic Characteristics of Qualitative Studies

* Information is gathered by:

  * Talking directly to people
  * Seeing how people behave and act in their real context
* Data are collected and interpreted by the researchers themselves.
* Multiple **open-ended forms of data** are collected.

  * Participants can freely share their ideas.
  * Participants are not restricted by predetermined scales or instruments.
* Researchers review the collected information and organize it into:

  * Codes
  * Themes
* Qualitative research typically begins with an **inductive approach**.

  * It moves from the bottom upward.
  * Researchers identify:

    * Patterns
    * Categories
    * Themes
  * These are gradually organized into increasingly general units of information.
* Researchers then look back at the data through the identified themes.

  * They determine whether additional information needs to be collected to support each theme.
  * This introduces a **deductive component** after the initial inductive process.
* The main focus is on understanding the **meaning that participants themselves attribute to the problem**.
* The initial research plan cannot always be tightly prescribed.

  * Some or all phases may change after data collection begins.
  * Research questions may change.
  * Forms of data collection may change.
  * The individuals being studied may change.
  * The sites being visited may change.
  * These changes can occur as the researcher develops a deeper understanding of the topic.
* The researcher's:

  * Personal background
  * Culture
  * Experiences
    can influence how the researcher interprets the data and the meaning assigned to it.
* Qualitative research develops a **holistic picture of the problem**.

  * It considers multiple perspectives.
  * It identifies many factors involved in a situation.
  * It does not necessarily follow a simple linear cause-and-effect model.
  * Instead, multiple factors may interact with one another in different ways within the actual setting. 

# When Qualitative Research is Suitable

* Qualitative research is suitable when:

  * There is no plan to quantify the collected data.
  * The researcher wants to understand **how and why**, rather than **whether and how many**.
  * The researcher wants to understand what the target population believes about a specific subject.
  * The researcher wants to obtain:

    * Reactions
    * Ideas
    * Feelings
      in the participants' own words.
  * The researcher wants freedom to probe beyond the initial responses to a question.
  * The researcher is studying sensitive or difficult topics where the **range and intensity of opinions** are important. 

# When Qualitative Research is Not Suitable

* Qualitative research is not suitable when:

  * Statistical data are required.
  * The study requires simple **yes/no answers**.
  * Rated or scaled answers are required.
  * Free expression of participants cannot be ensured.
  * Participants have problems with the social aspects of group participation.
  * There are language barriers. 

# Quantitative Research vs Qualitative Research

* **Purpose**

  * Quantitative research:

    * Confirms hypotheses about phenomena.
    * Usually tests an existing theory.
    * Focuses on deductive reasoning.
    * Moves from broad generalizations toward specific observations.
  * Qualitative research:

    * Explores phenomena.
    * Can contribute to developing a theory.
    * Focuses on inductive reasoning.
    * Moves from specific observations toward broad generalizations.

* **Examples**

  * Quantitative:

    * KAP surveys
    * Structured interviews
    * Structured observations
  * Qualitative:

    * In-depth interviews
    * Focus group discussions
    * Participant observation

* **Research instruments**

  * Quantitative research uses more rigid instruments.

    * Questionnaires
    * Surveys
    * Structured observations
  * These instruments help:

    * Categorize responses
    * Assign numerical values
    * Quantify variation
    * Predict causal relationships
    * Describe population characteristics
  * Qualitative research uses more flexible instruments.

    * Example: discussion guides 

# Differences in the Questions Asked

* **Quantitative research**

  * Answers questions such as:

    * How many?
    * How often?
  * Uses closed-ended questions.
  * Respondents are generally given predefined response choices.
  * Example:

    * “Was your child immunized for polio?”
    * Yes
    * No
    * Don't know
  * Closed-ended questions keep data organized.
  * However, they restrict the range of responses.
  * They may prevent the researcher from exploring the reason behind an answer.
  * For example, if someone answers “Don't know,” the researcher may not be able to ask why they do not know.

* **Qualitative research**

  * Answers questions such as:

    * Why?
    * How?
  * Uses open-ended questions.
  * Participants can provide detailed responses.
  * Open-ended responses can reveal:

    * Biases
    * Extent of knowledge
    * Gaps in thinking
  * Example:

    * “Why is immunization necessary?”
  * Such a question can produce a wide range of answers and can help identify the informational needs of the audience.

* Qualitative information may be recorded in open-ended textual formats such as:

  * Audiotapes
  * Videotapes
  * Field notes

* Quantitative studies generally follow the same study design from beginning to end.

* In qualitative research, elicitation and categorization of responses may be progressively modified depending on participant responses. 

# Differences in Data and Interpretation

* **Quantitative research**

  * Provides measurements of audience responses in numerical estimates.
  * Example:

    * A study may report that 40% of young adults reported always wearing masks in public places.
  * Deals primarily with:

    * Objective behavior
    * Measurable behavior
    * Knowledge
    * Attitudes
  * Usually involves a large sample size.
  * Large numbers of participants and interviewers can make quantitative research expensive.
  * Questions are generally straightforward to ask.
  * Results are generally straightforward to analyze.
  * Can draw firm conclusions and produce results that may be generalized to the population at large.

* **Qualitative research**

  * Provides an in-depth understanding of audience responses.
  * Example:

    * Young adults may not feel the need to wear masks because they learned that mortality from COVID-19 was lower among young adults.
  * Deals with:

    * Contextual aspects of human responses
    * Emotional aspects of human responses
  * Usually involves a smaller sample size.
  * Smaller numbers of participants and interviewers generally make it less expensive.
  * Results are more difficult to analyze.

    * They require:

      * Contemplation
      * Organization
      * Interpretation
  * Produces rich, detailed information.
  * Can provide answers to questions that researchers may not initially have thought to ask.
  * Provides insights into:

    * Attitudes
    * Beliefs
    * Motives
    * Concerns
    * Fears
    * Doubts
    * Behaviors
  * Can add deeper meaning to quantitative findings. 

# Research Design in Qualitative Research

* Qualitative research design is a **nonlinear process**.
* It is:

  * Emergent
  * Interdependent
* The different stages inform one another in a nonlinear manner.
* Design decisions can and should be:

  * Revisited
  * Reconsidered
    throughout the study.
* This is done based on:

  * What has been learned
  * What additional data are required
  * What is needed to answer the research question properly
* A research design must address three essential questions:

  * **What are you asking?**

    * The research question.
  * **How are you asking it?**

    * The method and/or theoretical position or perspective.
  * **What data will you need to provide a good answer?** 

# Ethnography

* Ethnography is one of the oldest qualitative research approaches.
* It has historical origins in **anthropology**.
* Its main focus is exploring the **“insider's point of view”**, known as the **emic perspective**.
* Ethnography can provide a greater understanding of behavior surrounding:

  * Health
  * Illness
* It can help understand how the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions may be influenced by:

  * Patients' cultural practices
  * Cultural beliefs
* Ethnography can also reveal how **ethnocentric assumptions made by professionals** may prevent effective health promotion.
* Ethnographers can use multiple strategies, including:

  * Participant observation
  * Interviews
  * Focus groups
  * Field notes
  * Video
  * Photography
  * Art
  * Other documents
* Different forms of ethnography have developed, including:

  * Institutional ethnography
  * Autoethnography 

# Institutional Ethnography

* Institutional ethnography can link the barriers and difficulties experienced by participants to specific features of health systems.
* It can demonstrate how institutional processes may be linked to health disparities.
* The lecture gives the example of:

  * Cancer disparities experienced by women.
* It can therefore help connect individual experiences with wider institutional and health-system processes. 

# Autoethnography

* Autoethnography draws upon ethnographic practices.
* It links the **personal to the cultural** through a self-narrative.
* Example:

  * A family's experience of dementia.
  * The approach can explore the relational aspects of these experiences. 

# Phenomenology

* Phenomenology seeks to:

  * Explore lived experiences
  * Describe lived experiences
  * Analyze lived experiences
* Its purpose is to develop **rich descriptions of people's experiences**.
* The researcher identifies a particular experience or phenomenon.

  * Example: pain.
* Participants who have experienced the phenomenon are recruited.
* The researcher attempts to develop a description of:

  * **What** the participants experienced
  * **How** they experienced it
* A key idea in phenomenology is that the audience should experience a **resonance or “ring of truth”** when hearing or reading the portrayal of the lived experience.
* Example:

  * Studying parents' experiences of carrying their neonatal baby from the hospital to the home environment.
  * Transferring a new baby may produce unexpected:

    * Anxiety
    * Pain
    * Worry
  * Findings from such research can have implications for healthcare teams providing care to:

    * Neonatal infants
    * Their families 

# Grounded Theory

* Grounded theory focuses on **deriving a theory or theories from systematically collected and analyzed data**.
* Data collection and data analysis occur **concurrently**.
* The process uses:

  * Induction
  * Deduction
  * Verification
* Grounded theory involves comparing and contrasting data.
* The purpose is to establish:

  * Categories
  * Relationships among categories
* These categories and relationships are then used to develop a theory that explains the phenomenon being studied.
* The process continues until **saturation** is reached.
* **Saturation** occurs when new cases do not bring any new information to light.
* Example:

  * Canales and Geller conducted a grounded theory study examining decision-making regarding mammography for breast cancer screening.
  * The study included:

    * Women who consistently received yearly mammograms
    * Women who were inconsistent or failed to receive annual mammograms
    * Breast cancer survivors
  * From the collected data, they developed a theory called **“Moving in Between Mammography.”**
  * The theory described the decision-making process and identified several factors influencing behavior. 

# Narrative Inquiry

* Narrative inquiry is a qualitative method in which **stories are understood as a social process**.
* Individuals construct meaning from their experiences through stories.
* Researchers:

  * Listen to narratives about an individual's experiences.
  * Record those narratives.
  * Discuss the meaning of the experience with the individual.
  * Interpret the individual's stories.
* Narratives can depend on:

  * Written words
  * Spoken words
  * Visual representations
* Information may be obtained through:

  * Interviews
  * Family stories
  * Journals
  * Field notes
  * Letters
  * Autobiographies
  * Conversations
  * Photographs
* Example:

  * Narrative methodology can explore the views of men and women about cardiovascular disease.
  * It can examine:

    * Emotions involved in personal experiences of cardiovascular disease
    * Social relationships involved in those experiences
  * Findings may reveal gendered and social influences on people's efforts to negotiate healing and rehabilitation. 

# Case Study

* A case study is used to generate an **in-depth, multifaceted understanding** of a complex issue.
* The issue is examined in its **real-life context**.
* Case studies can be used to study in detail:

  * Critical events
  * Interventions
  * Policy developments
  * Program-based service reforms
* The major strength is the detailed examination of complex issues within their actual context. 

# Observation

* Observational methods are often used together with other methods of data collection.
* Researchers observe what is happening around them, including:

  * Physical surroundings
  * Behaviors of people present
  * Interactions among people
* Researchers record:

  * Observations
  * Interpretations
    in the form of field notes.
* **Nonparticipant observation**

  * The investigator observes the behaviors of the people being studied.
  * Observation can be:

    * Covert
    * Overt
  * It takes place in real-life settings.
* Observation is a special skill.
* It requires careful planning of an **observational protocol**.
* The protocol helps ensure that:

  * Data are collected systematically.
  * Data are relevant to the research question.
* Bias must be considered when conducting observations. 

# Steps in Observational Data Collection

* **Select the site**

  * Choose the location where observation will occur.
  * Obtain permission to access the site when necessary.
* **Determine the focus of observation**

  * Decide who or what will be observed.
  * Determine when observation will occur.
  * Determine how long observation will continue.
* **Determine the role of the observer**

  * Decide whether the researcher will:

    * Participate actively
    * Remain a complete observer
    * Perform both roles
* **Develop observational protocols**

  * Establish how observations and field notes will be documented.
* **Plan entry and exit from the site**

  * Decide how the researcher will enter the setting.
  * Decide how the researcher will leave.
  * Consider whether someone needs to introduce the researcher to the setting or participants. 

# Participant Observation

* In participant observation, the researcher can function as:

  * A participant in the field
  * An observer
  * Both participant and observer
* Participant observation is useful for learning about:

  * Unexamined practices
  * Unexamined beliefs
* Example:

  * Participant observation of family group meetings can be used to study the impact of cancer on family relationships among patients.
  * Findings from such research can highlight the need for practitioners to focus on the **entire family** when designing interventions to help patients cope with cancer.
* Adams et al. used multiple methods to understand children's views about the hospital environment at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto.
* Their methods included:

  * Observational techniques
  * Interviews
  * Analysis of textual data
  * Analysis of visual data
* Their findings helped address a significant gap in understanding the relationship between:

  * Children's perceptions
  * The settings in which their healthcare occurs 

# Key Points to Remember

* Qualitative research is **naturalistic and interpretive**.
* It focuses on understanding the **meaning participants attribute to a problem**.
* It is particularly useful for understanding:

  * Beliefs
  * Reactions
  * Ideas
  * Feelings
  * Attitudes
  * Motives
  * Concerns
  * Fears
  * Doubts
  * Behaviors
* Qualitative research commonly begins with **inductive reasoning** and may subsequently use deductive reasoning to strengthen themes.
* The research plan can evolve as data collection progresses.
* Qualitative research design is **nonlinear, emergent, and interdependent**.
* Major qualitative approaches covered are:

  * Ethnography
  * Phenomenology
  * Grounded theory
  * Narrative inquiry
  * Case study
  * Participant observation
  * Nonparticipant observation
* **Ethnography** → insider's point of view and cultural context.

* **Narrative inquiry** → meaning constructed through stories.
* **Case study** → in-depth understanding of a complex issue in its real-life context.

* **Participant observation** → researcher participates and/or observes in the field.

# Choosing a Research Method

* The **aim of the study and the research question** direct:

  * The qualitative approach
  * The overall research design

* Different research questions about the same subject may require different qualitative methods.

* **Example: Emergency room practices**

  * Researchers interested in emergency room practices may use different qualitative approaches depending on what they want to understand.
  * If the objective is to understand **how cultural practices of the emergency department shape patient experiences**:

    * **Ethnography** would be an appropriate approach.
  * If the objective is to understand **the experiences of emergency patients waiting for admission in a hospital where there is a shortage of beds**:

    * **Phenomenology** would be a possible approach.
  * If the objective is to explain **the social processes influencing family members' decisions to access the emergency ward when experiencing chest pains**:

    * **Grounded theory** may be used.
  * If the objective is to understand **how patients make meaning of their experiences of accessing the emergency department during an acute illness episode**:

    * **Narrative methods** may be used. 

# Sampling in Qualitative Research

* In qualitative research designs, participants are selected based on their:

  * Relevance to the research topic
  * Relevance to the research question
  * Relevance to the conceptual framework
  * Relevance to the method being used
* Sampling is **not primarily guided by statistical representation**.
* The main purpose is to select participants who can provide the **greatest insights into the phenomenon of interest**.
* Several sampling procedures can therefore be used.

# Theoretical Sampling

* Participants are selected according to the **emerging theoretical framework**.
* Participants are selected to:

  * Elaborate emerging categories
  * Refine emerging categories
  * Refine the developing theory
* Theoretical sampling is a **core aspect of grounded theory**.

# Purposeful Sampling

* The researcher deliberately selects the participants who are considered the best sources of information.
* Selection is based on:

  * Quality of their insights
  * Knowledge of the phenomenon
  * Willingness to reflect upon the phenomenon of interest
* The central question is:

  * **“Who or what data can give me the most and the best information about my topic?”**

# Snowball or Chain Sampling

* Participants who are already included in the study are asked to:

  * Identify other people who meet the sampling criteria
  * Invite or refer those people to contact the researcher
* The process therefore proceeds through participant referrals.
* It is particularly useful when potential participants may be difficult to identify or reach directly.

# Convenience Sampling

* Participants are selected based on their **availability and accessibility**.
* It is one of the commonly used approaches in qualitative research. 

# Why Random Sampling is Generally Not Effective for Complex Qualitative Research

* Samples in qualitative studies are generally **small**.

* Because of the small sample size:

  * Sampling error is likely to be large.
  * Sampling biases, meaning differences between the sample and the population, are inevitable.

* A random sample provides the best opportunity to **generalize findings to a population**.

* However, random sampling is not an effective approach for understanding many complex issues related to human behaviour.

* **Reason: Characteristics of the population may not be known**

  * To select a truly random sample, the characteristics being studied should be known for the entire population.
  * In a complex qualitative study, this is rarely possible.

* **Reason: Qualitative characteristics may not be normally distributed**

  * Random sampling is likely to produce a representative sample only when the characteristics under study are normally distributed within the population.
  * Values, beliefs and attitudes are central to many qualitative investigations.
  * Available evidence indicates that these characteristics are not normally distributed.
  * Therefore, a probability sampling approach may be inappropriate.

* **Reason: People differ in their ability to provide information**

  * People are not equally capable of:

    * Observing their own behaviour
    * Understanding their behaviour
    * Interpreting their behaviour
    * Understanding the behaviour of people around them
  * Some informants can provide much better insight and understanding than others.
  * These particularly informative participants may be missed if random sampling is used. 

# Sample Size Determination in Qualitative Studies

* There is no universally fixed ideal number of participants for qualitative research.
* There may be an ideal number of participants for obtaining valid observations in certain methods, such as **focus groups**, but there are no universal rules specifying exactly how many observations are required to obtain valid results.
* In theory, sample size in qualitative research should be determined using the **principle of saturation**.

# Saturation

* Interviews are conducted until additional interviews provide **no additional insights** into the topic.
* Saturation occurs when:

  * Each category has become sufficiently **rich and thick**.
  * No new information is emerging.
* Therefore, the final sample size is not necessarily decided completely before data collection begins.
* Data collection can continue until the information becomes sufficiently saturated.

# Factors Affecting Appropriate Sample Size

* The appropriate sample size depends on:

  * **Nature of the topic**
  * **Amount of useful information obtained from each participant or source**
  * **Number of interviews conducted with each participant**

    * Some qualitative designs may involve interviewing the same participant more than once.
  * **Qualitative method and study design** 

# Selecting a Sample for a Qualitative Study

## Convenience Sampling

* Convenience sampling is described as one of the most widely used techniques.
* It involves selecting the **most accessible subjects**.
* Participants may be selected according to **preselected criteria** relevant to a particular research question.
* Examples include:

  * A class of students
  * A support group for people with cancer
  * People attending a vaccination clinic
* It is:

  * Less rigorous
  * Less costly in terms of:

    * Time
    * Effort
    * Money
* Sample sizes:

  * May be fixed before data collection.
  * May not be fixed before data collection.
* The final sample size can depend on:

  * Available resources
  * Available time
  * Objectives of the study 

# Venue-Based Time Sampling

* Venue-based time sampling involves recruiting participants at **venues where the population of interest gathers**.

* It involves multiple stages.

* **First-stage sampling**

  * Selection of the venue.

* **Second-stage sampling**

  * Selection of venue-and-day-time combinations, known as **VDTs**.

* **Third-stage sampling**

  * Selection of individuals within the sampled VDTs.

* This method is useful for **hard-to-access or hard-to-reach populations**.

* These populations may be difficult to recruit through traditional probability sampling techniques.

* Examples include:

  * Commercial sex workers
  * Injecting drug users
  * Seasonal migrants

* For such populations, convenience sampling may not be feasible.

* Venue-based time sampling uses a **two-stage sampling design**. 

# Venue-Based Time Sampling: Identifying Venues and Time Periods

* The first stage begins with **in-depth interviews**.
* These interviews are conducted to identify all potential venues that the target population might visit.
* Examples of potential venues include:

  * Street corners
  * Parks
  * Bars
  * Restaurants
* Once the complete set of potential venues has been identified:

  * Specific days and times that are likely to produce the greatest number of potential participants are identified.
* The number of members of the target population visiting each venue during different days and times is counted.
* This information is used to identify:

  * The best days
  * The best times
    for each venue.
* A final **sampling frame** is then developed.

  * It contains all potential venue-day-time combinations.
* A random sample can then be drawn from this sampling frame. 

# Recruitment in Venue-Based Time Sampling

* The second stage involves the **actual recruitment of participants at the selected venue**.
* Recruitment is conducted using a systematic approach.
* An imaginary or predetermined area is established within the venue.
* Potential participants who enter or cross into this area are:

  * Approached
  * Screened for eligibility
* If they meet the eligibility criteria:

  * They are asked to participate in the study.
* Systematic selection at the venue helps reduce the **selection bias** that can occur with convenience sampling.

# Key Points

* The **aim of the study and research question** determine the appropriate qualitative approach and overall design.
* Different research questions require different qualitative approaches.
* **Ethnography**

  * Focuses on cultural practices and the insider's point of view.
  * Uses the emic perspective.
* **Phenomenology**

  * Can be used to understand people's lived experiences.
* **Grounded theory**

  * Can be used to explain social processes and develop theory from data.
* **Narrative methods**

  * Can be used to understand how people make meaning from their experiences.
* Qualitative sampling focuses on **relevance and information richness**, rather than statistical representation.
* Four important qualitative sampling procedures are:

  * Theoretical sampling
  * Purposeful sampling
  * Snowball or chain sampling
  * Convenience sampling
* **Theoretical sampling** is particularly important in grounded theory.
* **Purposeful sampling** selects participants who can provide the most useful information.
* **Snowball sampling** uses participant referrals.
* **Convenience sampling** selects participants based on availability.
* Qualitative studies generally use **small samples**.
* Sample size is commonly guided by **saturation**.
* Saturation occurs when additional data provide no new information.
* Appropriate sample size depends on:

  * Topic
  * Information obtained from participants
  * Number of interviews per participant
  * Research method
  * Study design
* **Venue-based time sampling** is useful for hard-to-reach populations.
* Venue-based time sampling identifies:

  * Relevant venues
  * Relevant days
  * Relevant times
  * Individuals within selected venue-day-time combinations.
* Systematic recruitment at venues can reduce selection bias associated with convenience sampling.

  * Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and In-Depth Interviews (IDIs)
  * Ground rules, Do’s and Don’ts for interviewing
  * Visual methods 

# Interview Designs

* There are **three main interview designs**:

  * Structured interview
  * Semi-structured interview
  * Unstructured/open-ended interview

## Structured Interview

* Follows the **same sequence of questions** for every participant.
* The same questions are asked in the **same order**.
* Can include:

  * Open-ended questions
  * Closed-ended questions
* Questions and wording are **predetermined**.
* Predetermined questions and wording:

  * Reduce the risk of the interviewer's own biases.
  * Provide consistency across different interviews.
* Particularly useful when:

  * More than one interviewer is involved.
  * Consistency between interviews is important.
* A major limitation is its highly structured nature.
* The interviewer has **little freedom** to:

  * Explore participants' responses in greater depth.
  * Encourage participants to expand on their answers.
  * Use probes according to the participant's response.
* Therefore, although structured interviews provide consistency, they provide less flexibility for exploring unexpected information. 

## Semi-Structured Interview

* Contains **specific predetermined items or questions**.
* The interviewer is nevertheless given some flexibility.
* Interviewers can explore participant responses related to the research questions.
* Questions generally focus on:

  * Selected topics
  * Selected hypotheses
  * Issues identified from the **Review of Literature (RoL)**
* The interview is therefore **not completely predetermined**.
* It provides a balance between:

  * Consistency through predetermined questions
  * Flexibility through exploration and probing. 

## Unstructured/Open-Ended Interview

* It is the **most common type of qualitative interview**.
* Researchers may still prepare an interview guide.
* The guide generally consists of:

  * Planned topics
  * Areas that need to be explored
* However, the interview itself is not rigidly predetermined.
* The purpose is to give the participant an opportunity to express and explain their experiences and views in depth.
* It provides the greatest flexibility for exploring issues that emerge during the interview. 

# Interviewing

* The first priority is to **establish rapport** with the participant.
* The researcher should create a sense of:

  * Trust
  * Comfort
  * Openness
* Rapport can be affected by other factors, including **gender**.
* Begin the conversation with:

  * Less sensitive topics
  * Benign topics
* Gradually move the conversation toward:

  * More sensitive issues
  * More complex issues
* During the interview, identify responses that provide opportunities for **in-depth probing**.

## Probing

* Probing is a technique in which **verbal or nonverbal communication** is used to obtain more information from the participant.
* Probes encourage participants to:

  * Explain further
  * Clarify their responses
  * Provide additional information
  * Go deeper into their experiences

### Types and Examples of Probes

* **Directive question**

  * Example: “Could you tell me a little more about that?”
* **Echo probe**

  * The interviewer paraphrases what the interviewee has just said.
* **Minimal verbal responses**

  * “Uh-huh”
  * “I see”
  * “Yes”
* Probes should be **as neutral as possible**.
* Neutrality is important because probes should not introduce interviewer bias.
* If a participant says:

  * “I don't know.”
  * “I am not sure.”
* A neutral **clarification probe** can be used:

  * “There are no right or wrong answers to these questions; we are only interested in finding out how you feel about this.”
* **Nonverbal probes** are also effective.
* Examples include:

  * Nodding the head
  * Remaining silent
* Silence can give the interviewee time to:

  * Reflect
  * Think
  * Prepare their next thought. 

# Deciding the Interview Protocol

* Consider the **degree of detail required** to fully answer the research question.

* An unstructured/open-ended interview provides the greatest opportunity to obtain **in-depth detail**.

* However, to take advantage of an unstructured interview:

  * The interviewer needs appropriate training.
  * The interviewer must be well prepared.

* Consider:

  * How much is already known about the topic.
  * What still needs to be learned.

* Consider the level of simplicity required during **data analysis and coding**.

  * More unstructured data generally results in a more complex analytical process.
  * Open-ended interviews therefore require more complex coding and analysis.

## Practical Issues

* Decide how the interview will be recorded.
* Consider whether:

  * Digital recording will be used.
  * The interviewer is familiar with the recording device.
  * The interviewer will maintain field notes.

## Process Issues

* Determine whether the interview guide will be **pilot tested**.
* Decide the order in which questions should be asked.
* Consider language-related issues while developing the interview questions.
* Questions should be appropriate to the language and context of the participants.

## Data Management Issues

* Decide where recordings will be stored.
* Decide where:

  * Field notes
  * Demographic information
    will be stored.
* Consider whether participants will receive an **honorarium**.
* Decide the form of honorarium:

  * Cash
  * Gift
* Consider what happens if a participant:

  * Withdraws from the interview.
  * Withdraws from the study.
* Determine:

  * What happens to the honorarium after withdrawal.
  * What happens to the participant's data. 

# Focus Group

* The term **“Focus Group”** was coined in 1991 by marketing and psychological expert **Ernest Dichter**.
* The term originally described meetings involving a limited group of participants for the purpose of discussion.
* A focus group is:

  * A small group of carefully selected participants.
  * Participants contribute to an open discussion.
* Researchers carefully select participants so that the group represents the larger population they are attempting to target.
* Focus groups are the **most common type of group interview used in qualitative research**.
* A **moderator**:

  * Manages the discussion.
  * Helps ensure legitimate results.
  * Helps reduce bias in the discussion.
* A typical focus group consists of:

  * **6–10 people**
  * Usually around **8 participants**
* Participants meet to explore and discuss a particular topic.
* Group members share:

  * Feedback
  * Opinions
  * Knowledge
  * Insights
* The moderator takes notes regarding:

  * The discussion
  * Opinions of group members.
* Careful participant selection is essential because the choice of group members can affect the results.
* Focus groups involve clear **group dynamics and interaction**.
* Group interaction is therefore an important focus of:

  * Data generation
  * Data analysis
* The purpose of a focus group is to better understand how people:

  * Think
  * Feel
    about a designated topic. 

# When to Conduct Focus Group Discussions

* Focus groups are a **flexible data-gathering technique**.
* They can be:

  * Time efficient
  * Cost efficient
* Focus groups can generate more ideas and issues about a topic than an individual conversation may generate.
* This happens because:

  * Participants build on each other's ideas.
  * Participants share experiences.
  * Interaction stimulates additional thoughts and perspectives.

## Focus Groups Can Be Used When

* Looking for a **range of ideas** that people have about a topic.
* Particularly useful when ideas emerge through **group interaction**.
* **Pilot testing ideas**.
* Obtaining information needed to design a larger quantitative study.

  * For example, identifying appropriate language to use in a research instrument.
* Seeking insight into **quantitative data that have already been obtained**.
* Conducting **participatory action research** for:

  * Decision making
  * Consensus seeking 

## Potential Limitations of Focus Groups

* Some participants may feel uncomfortable:

  * Expressing their views in front of others.
  * Describing their experiences in a group.
* This is particularly relevant when discussing **sensitive topics**.
* The researcher spends less time overall with the total number of participants compared with individual interviews.
* **Group influences** can distort an individual's actual opinion.
* Confidentiality has limitations.

  * Participants may disclose aspects of the group discussion or group process outside the group.
* Therefore, focus groups may not be appropriate when:

  * The topic is highly sensitive.
  * Participants may be unwilling to speak openly in front of others.
  * Individual privacy is essential. 

# Ground Rules for Interviewing

* Greet the respondent at the beginning of the interview in a **culturally appropriate manner**.
* Explain:

  * The purpose of the interview.
  * What the interview involves.
* Obtain **informed consent**.
* Explain how information will be recorded.
* Obtain permission before:

  * Audio recording
  * Video recording
* Arrange **comfortable seating** to facilitate communication.
* A **note taker** may be involved.
* Use **ice breakers** to help participants feel comfortable.
* Begin with a topic that:

  * Is not sensitive.
  * Is important to the respondent.
* This helps create:

  * An informal atmosphere
  * A friendly atmosphere
  * A natural flow of ideas and opinions.
* Be an **active listener**.
* Look at the informant's face rather than constantly looking at the interview guide.
* Always behave in a **culturally sensitive** manner.
* Pick up phrases used by the informant and use those phrases when framing subsequent questions.
* Avoid:

  * Giving opinions about what the informant says.
  * Making judgments about the informant's responses.
* Treat the informant as an **equal**. 

# Ground Rules for Interviewing: Questions and Discussion

* Use **open-ended questions** rather than closed questions.
* Open-ended questions allow participants to provide detailed responses instead of limiting them to:

  * “Yes”
  * “No”
* Avoid **leading questions**.
* Allow the discussion to flow freely.
* At the same time, make sure that all important topics are covered.
* Use **probing questions** to:

  * Clarify points.
  * Encourage further explanation.
* If informants raise issues that are not included in the interview guide:

  * Respond to those issues.
  * Explore them through appropriate probing.
* At the end:

  * Thank the informant.
  * Give the informant an opportunity to ask additional questions. 

# Quality of Interview

## Strategies to Improve Interview Quality

### Active Listening

* Listen carefully to what the respondent says.
* Rephrase what was said to ensure that the researcher has completely understood what the respondent intends to communicate.
* Active listening helps:

  * Confirm understanding.
  * Avoid misinterpretation.
  * Encourage participants to elaborate.

### Patience

* Do not rush the respondent.
* Allow the respondent to speak freely.
* At the same time:

  * Guide the conversation toward important issues.
  * Gently refocus the conversation if it moves away from the research topic.
* In-depth interview questions should be viewed as **invitations to share a story**.
* The respondent should be given the opportunity to tell their story.

### Flexibility

* Be open to slight deviations from the planned topic.
* Such deviations may require:

  * Rearranging questions.
  * Reordering questions.
  * Developing new questions during the interview.
* Flexibility is important because participants may introduce useful information that was not anticipated in the original interview plan. 

# Visual Methods

## Photovoice

* **Photovoice** is a qualitative research method.

* It is used in **community-based participatory research**.

* It gathers:

  * Photographs taken by participants.
  * Narratives provided by participants.

* The purpose is to translate participants' experiences into **actionable knowledge**.

* Photovoice:

  * Supports qualitative discussion.
  * Captures evidence.
  * Produces data that are rich and meaningful.
  * Develops community skills.
  * Develops community understanding.
  * Can be empowering for participants.
  * Can be transformative for participants.

* Photovoice can be used to obtain:

  * New insights.
  * New perspectives.

* It can raise awareness about:

  * Hidden issues.
  * Overlooked issues.
  * Important aspects of a particular community.

* Participants are asked to express their point of view or represent their community by:

  * Taking photographs.
  * Photographing scenes that highlight the themes being investigated. 

## Community Mapping

* Community mapping is another **visual qualitative method**.
* Community members are invited to participate.
* Participants are divided into groups.
* Each group is asked to draw maps of its environment.
* The maps should include:

  * Resources.
  * Sites of importance.
  * Locations relevant to the problem being studied.
* After the maps are created:

  * Groups compare their maps.
  * Groups discuss the maps.
  * Similarities are identified.
  * Differences are identified.
* This process can help researchers understand how community members perceive:

  * Their environment.
  * Available resources.
  * Important sites.
  * Issues associated with the research problem.

## Steps in Qualitative Data Analysis 

## Qualitative Data

* Data collected through qualitative studies are mostly **descriptive data**.
* Descriptive data can take different forms:

  * Words
  * Pictures
  * Videos
  * Notes
  * Charts
  * Records
  * Narratives
* Examples of qualitative data include:

  * Interview transcripts
  * Recorded observations

    * Video observations
    * Participatory observations
  * Texts and documents
  * Multimedia or public-domain sources
  * Photographs
  * Lay accounts
* Unlike quantitative data, qualitative data are **not represented primarily in numerical terms**.
* If some numerical interpretation is provided in qualitative research, its purpose is generally to **emphasize a pattern in the data**, rather than to represent the data in the same way as quantitative research. 

## Qualitative Analytic Reasoning

* Qualitative studies usually rely on **reasoning processes** to interpret and structure the meanings that can be derived from data.
* Analysis is an explicit step in which the researcher:

  * Conceptually interprets the data set.
  * Transforms raw data into a coherent depiction of the thing being studied.
* An important distinction exists between:

  * **Explanation** – explaining how something operates.
  * **Interpretation** – understanding why something operates in the way that it does.
* This distinction helps differentiate quantitative and qualitative analytic processes.
* In qualitative research:

  * Data collection and analysis are **concurrent**.
  * Analysis informs and guides additional data collection.
  * Therefore, researchers do not necessarily complete all data collection before beginning analysis.
* Many computer programs are available for qualitative data analysis.
* However, these programs are essentially **aids for sorting and organizing qualitative data**.
* They are not capable of replacing the researcher's:

  * Intellectual processes
  * Conceptualization
  * Interpretation
  * Transformation of data into meaningful findings. 

## Qualitative Data Analysis

### Data Management

* Qualitative data should first be **clearly organized**.
* Original data and subsequent analysis should be recorded in a file.
* The file should include important identifying and defining information such as:

  * Dates
  * Locations
  * Individual or group characteristics
  * Interviewer characteristics
  * Other defining features
* Digital recordings of:

  * Interviews
  * Visual materials
    should be reviewed to ensure that the data being analyzed correspond to the **original observations**.
* If ethical agreements require that names or identifying characteristics should not be recorded:

  * All individual names must be removed from the final transcriptions **before analysis begins**.
* When textual data analysis software is used:

  * Careful monitoring of data files is essential.
  * This is particularly important when **multiple coders** are involved. 

### Data Condensation

* **Data condensation** is the process of:

  * Selecting
  * Focusing
  * Simplifying
  * Abstracting
    the original data and transforming them into a data set that can be analyzed.
* A substantial amount of time in qualitative research is spent **after fieldwork is completed**.
* Transcription can be highly time-consuming:

  * One hour of a taped individual in-depth interview may require a full day to transcribe.
  * Additional time may be needed if translation is required.
  * Focus group interviews can take even longer to process.
* Every stage of data condensation involves multiple decisions.
* Close supervision is required to maintain the correct balance between:

  * Preserving the original language
  * Maintaining clarity in the translated version
  * Making the material suitable for analysis
* The original characteristics of participants' speech should not be unnecessarily changed.
* For example:

  * Discussions among groups with little or no formal education should not appear as though they were conversations among university graduates after transcription and translation.
* Judgment is necessary when deciding:

  * Which terms should be translated.
  * Which terms should remain in the vernacular.
* Some vernacular terms may contain meanings and richness that cannot be adequately captured by an English equivalent. 

### Example: Domains and Qualitative Data

**Fear**

* Myths about HIV transmission included beliefs such as:

  * People should not socially interact with spouses of PLWHA (people living with HIV/AIDS) to avoid infection.
  * PLWHA should not be allowed to cook food for others.
* Acts reflecting fear included:

  * A PLWHA was not allowed to watch television at the local youth club.
  * A PLWHA was not allowed to use the village pond.

**Blame and Judgement**

* Moral connotations included:

  * PLWHAs who acquired HIV infection through sexual promiscuity should be punished.
  * Women having the infection were blamed for destroying the family.
  * Men were not similarly blamed.

**Shame**

* HIV was associated with shame because it was believed to have been acquired through sexual promiscuity. 

### Data Display

* After condensation, data need to be **organized and displayed**.
* The purpose of data display is to:

  * Facilitate comparison.
  * Increase the depth of conclusions.
  * Increase the detail of conclusions.
* Data displays can range from:

  * Full verbatim interview transcripts
  * Bulleted summaries of interview notes
* A useful display format is an **overview chart**.
* In an overview chart:

  * Key themes or research questions are listed in rows.
  * Individual informant or group characteristics are listed across columns.
* Overview charts allow **easy and systematic comparison of results**.
* The example presented is based on research by **Panda, Samiran, et al.**, titled *Exploring stigma in low HIV prevalence settings in rural West Bengal, India: Identification of intervention considerations*, published in *Journal of Mixed Methods Research*, 9(4), 362–385. 

## Drawing and Verifying Conclusions

### Analysis

**Thematic Analysis**

* Thematic analysis involves:

  * Careful reading
  * Systematic reading
  * Repeated reading of text
* The purpose is to identify:

  * Consistent themes
  * Interconnections emerging from the data
* Repeated reading can reveal:

  * New themes
  * New connections
  * Deeper meanings
* The researcher should read the **full interview text multiple times** before subdividing it according to coded themes.
* Reading the complete text first is necessary to appreciate:

  * The full context
  * The flow of the interview
* Only after understanding the broader context should the researcher subdivide and extract coded sections of text.

**Content Analysis**

* Content analysis involves the:

  * Description
  * Categorization
    of common words, phrases and ideas in qualitative data.
* It can **quantify qualitative information** by systematically:

  * Sorting information
  * Comparing information
  * Summarizing information
* Content analysis involves:

  * **Conceptual analysis** – more numbers-based.
  * **Relational analysis** – focuses on relationships between concepts and how they are connected.

**Textual Analysis**

* Textual analysis examines:

  * Content
  * Structure
  * Design
    of texts.
* Example:

  * Content available on social media platforms.
* Aspects that may be examined include:

  * Frequencies
  * Magnitude
  * Structures
  * Processes
  * Causes
  * Consequences 

### Coding

* Coding involves developing **codes for labeling sections of text**.
* The purpose is to allow **selective retrieval** during later stages of:

  * Analysis
  * Verification
* **Structural coding**

  * Follows the structure of the interview guide.
* **Thematic coding**

  * Is based on themes that emerge from the data.
* A potential problem in qualitative research is **shift and drift in codes**:

  * Codes may change over time.
  * Different coders may interpret codes differently.
* To avoid this problem, qualitative investigators should develop a **standard codebook**.
* The codebook should contain:

  * Written definitions of codes.
  * Rules specifying when a code should start.
  * Rules specifying when a code should stop.
* Coding is a **repetitive process**.
* As the researcher repeatedly reads the data:

  * New codes may emerge.
  * New codes are incorporated with existing codes.
* Development and refinement of the codebook is an **integral part of qualitative analysis**.
* Codes can also be assigned using computerized systems. 

### Analyzing and Writing Memos

* Answers to the research question emerge during:

  * Coding
  * Selective text retrieval
* Similarities within and between coding categories can be:

  * Extracted
  * Compared systematically
* **Memoing** is the process of writing notes or memos about thoughts related to the analysis of one's data.
* Memos can include:

  * Personal reflections
  * Personal impressions
  * Phrases or ideas that are surprising
  * Similarities in responses
  * Distinct differences in responses
  * Additional questions raised by the data
* Researchers can record these reactions directly in the text when the software or document allows it.
* For example:

  * A comment may be associated with a highlighted phrase in a Word document.
* Memos record:

  * Evolving insights
  * Emerging patterns
  * Relationships between the data and the original research questions
* Memos may contain reflective notes about what the researcher is learning from the data.
* This may involve the:

  * Fieldworker
  * Data coder
  * Analyst
* Memos accumulate as written ideas or records, such as journals, about:

  * Concepts
  * Relationships between concepts
* These accumulated ideas help initiate further thinking about the data.
* This can lead to:

  * Further coding
  * Deeper understanding
  * Better interpretation of the data. 

## Important Points About Qualitative Data Analysis

* Data analysis is the **most complex part of all phases of a qualitative study**.
* Merely creating a database is **not sufficient**.
* To transform raw data into new knowledge, a qualitative researcher must actively engage in demanding analytic processes throughout **all phases of research**.
* Important aspects of analysis include:

  * Reading
  * Understanding
  * Interpreting
* Qualitative research analysis requires:

  * Preparation
  * Careful planning
* Researchers should take sufficient time to **familiarize themselves with the data before beginning analysis**.
* Once analysis begins, researchers should have strategies to capture and record:

  * Their reactions to the data.
  * Their developing understanding of the collective meaning of the data.
* Qualitative research is concerned not only with the **end results**, but also with the **process through which the researcher arrives at those results**.
* Qualitative conclusions involve **subjective judgment**.
* Therefore, it is beneficial to involve more than one analyst.
* Multiple analysts can:

  * Discuss conclusions.
  * Debate interpretations.
  * Compare perspectives.
  * Reach consensus before final conclusions are made.

## Common Concepts of Qualitative Methods

* Qualitative researchers study things in their **natural setting**.
* They attempt to:

  * Make sense of phenomena.
  * Interpret phenomena in terms of the **meanings people bring to them**.
* The common purpose of qualitative methods is to create a model for **in-depth understanding** of the links among different elements.
* Qualitative research explores an order or relationship that may be **invisible during ordinary examination**.
* In qualitative research:

  * The **process** is particularly powerful.
  * The emphasis is not primarily on the **product**, such as mathematically estimated results.
* Qualitative research uses **inductive reasoning**:

  * Researchers begin with field data.
  * They explore individualities and different experiences.
  * Information is collected gradually.
  * The accumulated information helps build a **plausible theory**.
* **Emic perspective:**

  * Interpretations should be made from the **interviewees' perspective**.
  * Interpretation should not primarily represent the researcher's perspective.
  * It should also not simply represent the perspective found in existing literature.
* The emphasis is therefore on understanding phenomena from the perspective and meanings of the people being studied. 

## Broad Types of Qualitative Research

* The broad types of qualitative research include:

  * **Exploratory research**
  * **Descriptive research**
  * **Comparative research** 

## Strengths and Weaknesses of Qualitative Research

* Qualitative research has several **strengths and weaknesses**.
* The material identifies strengths and weaknesses as an important area for consideration when using qualitative methods. 

## Limitations of Qualitative Research

* Possible **small sample sizes**.
* **Potential bias in answers**.
* **Self-selection bias**.
* Possibility of **poor questions from researchers**.
* Another important limitation is the **Hawthorne Effect**.

### Hawthorne Effect

* The Hawthorne Effect refers to a situation in which:

  * Participants in behavioral studies **change their behavior or performance in response to being observed**.
* Therefore, the act of observing participants can itself influence their behavior. 

## Challenges with Qualitative Research

* Important challenges include:

  * **Choosing an appropriate method**.
  * **Avoiding bias**.
  * **Ensuring accuracy and consistency**.
  * **Managing large amounts of data**.
* Qualitative research can generate substantial amounts of information, making data management an important challenge. 

## Multicentric Qualitative Research and Quality Assurance

* Multicentric qualitative research involves researchers collecting data in **different/local settings**.
* Each researcher may collect information:

  * In their local setting.
  * Using the **native language** of the participants.
* However, a **common language for coding and analysis** is required.

  * This facilitates sharing information across different research sites.
  * It also helps disseminate research findings to wider audiences.
* Researchers should develop **codes for data analysis in English** so that the codes can be shared between research centres.
* At the same time:

  * Language fidelity and fluency of participants must be respected.
  * Analysis should be conducted in the **original language of the interviews** by the local research teams.
* Researchers at each centre generate a list of **narrative codes** while completing:

  * Stakeholder interviews.
  * Focus groups.
* The codes generated at each centre are circulated among researchers from the different centres.
* The individual lists of codes are developed into a **shared codebook**.
* Development of the shared codebook can occur through:

  * Face-to-face meetings.
  * Conference calls.
  * Electronic mail correspondence.
* The final result is a **consensus codebook**. 

## Creating a Codebook for Data Analysis

* **Coding** is an interpretative process.
* During coding:

  * Conceptual labels are assigned to the data.
* **Codes** are words or devices used for identifying **themes**.
* Overall quality assurance should include a **data analysis plan**.
* The analysis should use a **consensus approach**.
* A consensus approach to the analysis process helps maximise **intercoder reliability** when coding open-ended data.
* Intercoder reliability is particularly important when several researchers or research centres are involved in analysing qualitative information. 

## Methodological Issues in Multicentre Qualitative Research

* Multicentre studies present a particular **methodological challenge** in qualitative research.
* The reason is that:

  * Each location has its own local context.
  * Local context adds complexity.
  * Local context creates opportunities for introducing **confounding variables**.
* **Generalizability** means extending research findings from the study population to the population at large.
* Generalizability is generally **not possible** in the conventional sense because:

  * Qualitative studies generally use small samples.
  * The study may involve a small group of people or a small number of cases.
  * The research context is generally not representative of the larger population.
* However, interventions may still be applicable to other distinct populations.
* Investigators may:

  * Learn from the findings of the original study.
  * Transfer those findings.
  * Apply them to other settings where appropriate. 

## Social Data as Qualitative Data

### Why Think of Social Data as Qualitative Data?

* What people share on social platforms can consist of:

  * Rich stories.
  * Deep personal experiences.
  * Unstructured information.
  * Images.
  * Videos.
  * Text.
* These forms of information can be considered **qualitative data**.
* Thinking of social data as qualitative data opens up a new set of tools for analysing it.
* **Primary qualitative research** allows researchers to explore a subject in **depth**.
* **Social qualitative data** allows researchers to explore the **breadth** of a subject.
* The value of social data comes from its **scale**.
* Researchers can observe the behaviours and experiences of **thousands of people**.
* This can provide a more comprehensive picture of the phenomenon being studied. 

## Applying Qualitative Methods to Social Data

* **Grounded Theory** is well suited to the analysis of social data.
* Appropriate analytical tools should be developed.
* These can include:

  * **Discourse analysis**
  * **Narrative analysis**
  * **Thematic analysis**
  * Other suitable analytical approaches.
* The data should be **codified**.
* The data can also be **quantified**:

  * Numbers can be used to explore frequencies.
  * Numbers can be used to identify patterns.
  * Quantification provides an outline of the story contained in the data.
* After establishing the broad picture, researchers can conduct a **deep dive**.
* During the deep dive:

  * The outline is filled with detail.
  * Thematic analysis can be used.
  * Narrative analysis can be used.
  * These methods help identify and understand the **meaning within the data**.
* **Synthesis** is the final step:

  * Different elements are brought together.
  * The elements are combined to complete the overall picture. 

## Case Study: How the British Red Cross Learnt to Talk About Loneliness

### The Question

* The **British Red Cross** works to:

  * Connect human crisis with human kindness.
  * Encourage resilient communities where people look after each other.
* As part of this work, it sought to:

  * Raise public awareness about loneliness.
  * Improve public understanding of loneliness.
  * Provide suitable services.
  * Influence policy concerning loneliness.
* The central issue was therefore understanding loneliness and communicating about it effectively. 

### The Approach

* Loneliness is often:

  * Hidden.
  * Rarely spoken about openly.
* There is a **stigma surrounding loneliness**.
* Understanding of loneliness can be shaped by **dated stereotypes**.
* Social media provides a degree of anonymity.
* This anonymity can:

  * Give people a safe space to talk.
  * Allow people to discuss loneliness more openly.
  * Provide an opportunity to better appreciate and understand loneliness.
* Social media therefore became useful for understanding experiences that people might not openly discuss in face-to-face settings. 

### Scoping

* The British Red Cross first worked to understand the **nature of the challenge**.
* The scoping process considered:

  * What was already known.
  * What activities and strategies were already in place.
  * What had previously been tried.
* From a technical perspective, time was spent determining **exactly what was meant by loneliness**.
* This definition was necessary so that the audience profile could be mapped onto the available social data. 

### Data Collection

* A **social listening partner** was used to identify conversations among people dealing with loneliness.
* There is a common misconception that social data mainly consists of:

  * People showing off.
  * People arguing.
* However, social data can often be much more **reflective of actual life**.
* The anonymity of online forums can make people feel comfortable sharing:

  * Very personal experiences.
  * Personal aspects of their lives.
* This made social data useful for understanding people's real experiences of loneliness. 

## Key Points to Remember

* Qualitative research studies phenomena in their **natural settings**.
* It focuses on the **meanings people bring to phenomena**.
* **Emic** means understanding and interpreting experiences from the **participants' perspective**.
* Qualitative research emphasises the **process** rather than mathematically estimated results.
* It commonly uses **inductive reasoning** to gradually build a plausible theory.
* Broad types include:

  * Exploratory.
  * Descriptive.
  * Comparative.
* Major limitations include:

  * Small sample sizes.
  * Bias in answers.
  * Self-selection bias.
  * Hawthorne Effect.
  * Poorly designed questions.
* Major challenges include:

  * Choosing the appropriate method.
  * Avoiding bias.
  * Ensuring accuracy and consistency.
  * Managing large amounts of data.
* Multicentric qualitative research requires:

  * Local-language data collection.
  * Shared coding procedures.
  * A common codebook.
  * Consensus among researchers.
  * Attention to intercoder reliability.
* Local context in multicentre research can introduce **complexity and confounding variables**.
* Conventional generalizability is usually not possible because qualitative samples and contexts are generally not representative of larger populations.
* Findings may nevertheless be **transferred and applied to other appropriate settings**.
* Social media data can be treated as qualitative data because it contains:

  * Stories.
  * Images.
  * Videos.
  * Text.
  * Rich and unstructured experiences.
* Social qualitative data provides **breadth through scale**, while primary qualitative research provides **depth**.
* Grounded Theory can be used for social data analysis.
* Important analytical approaches include:

  * Discourse analysis.
  * Narrative analysis.
  * Thematic analysis.
* Social qualitative analysis can involve:

  * Codification.
  * Quantification.
  * Identification of frequencies and patterns.
  * Deep thematic and narrative analysis.
  * Synthesis.
* The British Red Cross loneliness case demonstrates how social data can help understand a sensitive issue that people may hesitate to discuss openly.
