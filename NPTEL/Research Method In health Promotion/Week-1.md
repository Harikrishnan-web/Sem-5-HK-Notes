## **Health**

* The WHO defines health as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity; the lecture also connects this with the ability to lead a socially and economically productive life. 
* The biomedical definition treats health as the absence of disease or disability. 
* The biomedical definition is easier to measure because it focuses on whether disease or disability is present or absent. 
* The WHO definition is broader and more holistic, but it is also criticized for being too broad and difficult to measure. 
* The transcript reinforces that the WHO definition is more useful for health promotion because it opens the way to understanding multiple determinants of health, not just disease status. 

## **Determinants of Health**

* Determinants are factors that decisively affect the nature or outcome of something. 
* Health is influenced by more than health care services; many other factors also shape health status. 
* The context in which a person lives is very important for both health status and quality of life. 
* Determinants of health are the factors that influence the health status of individuals or populations. 

### **Main Determinants Listed by WHO**

* **Social and economic environment**

  * Includes culture and traditions.
  * Shapes everyday life, opportunities, and health-related choices. 
* **Physical environment**

  * Includes safe water, clean air, healthy workplaces, safe houses, and good roads.
  * These conditions contribute directly to good health. 
* **Individual characteristics**

  * Includes genetics, gender, behaviors, and practices.
  * Examples given include diet, physical activity, smoking, drinking, and coping skills for life stress. 

## **Health Promotion**

* Health promotion gained importance as attention moved toward the social determinants of health and the broader social and environmental factors that influence health. This shift helped shape the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion at the First International Conference on Health Promotion in Ottawa in 1986. 
* Health promotion is defined as the process of enabling people to increase control over and improve their health. 
* It goes beyond individual behavior and includes social and environmental interventions. 
* The lecture presents health promotion as a paradigm shift because earlier health thinking focused mainly on treating disease, while health promotion emphasizes improving health and well-being and preventing disease. 
* The transcript adds that health promotion is not only about intervention after illness begins; it also prevents disease in the pre-pathogenesis phase and supports healthy practices already present in communities. 

## **Health Promotion and the Determinants of Health**

* Health promotion works through **health education** and **health protection**, and health protection operates through healthy public policies and programs. 
* **Health education**

  * Involves educational efforts to change harmful behavior and lifestyles.
  * Examples include open defecation and addiction as behaviors/lifestyles that adversely affect health.
  * Encourages use of available health services.
  * Promotes participation in preventive services such as cervical cancer screening. 
* **Health protection**

  * Addresses policies and regulations that are preventive in nature.
  * Example given: a regulatory approach to prevent over-speeding. 
* The combined effort of health education and health protection is needed. 
* Health promotion improves health by influencing lifestyles, health services, and especially environments, including physical, cultural, and socioeconomic circumstances. 
* The transcript emphasizes that health promotion changes determinants in a positive direction so that healthy practices are encouraged and harmful practices are reduced. 

## **Health Promotion Approach to Address Health Disparities and Inequities**

* Health promotion reduces inequities by improving health literacy and creating supportive environments. 
* The two major approaches shown are:

  * **Health Education**: improving individual knowledge and skills.
  * **Advocacy for Public Health Policies**: creating a supportive environment. 
* These approaches modulate the determinants of health through health promotion. 
* Improved health outcomes and reduction in health inequities are linked; one supports the other. 

## **Scope of Health Promotion**

### **Empowerment**

* Empowerment is a central scope of health promotion.
* It means putting people in charge of their own health so they can actively make decisions affecting it. 
* Empowerment is connected with social and economic development and status. 
* The transcript gives a gender example: if only men make health-related decisions in a household, women are not empowered.
* Health promotion aims to reduce such inequities so that women can take decisions related to their own health, such as contraceptive choices. 

### **Need-Based Interventions**

* Health promotion carries out need-based policy, organizational, economic, regulatory, and environmental interventions. 
* Without policy support for social change, health education alone may not be enough to help people achieve their health goals. 
* The transcript gives examples of economic interventions such as incentives under health programs, where beneficiaries receive monetary support for performing desired practices. 
* Regulatory interventions include laws on speed limits, helmet use, and seat belt use. 
* Environmental interventions may involve reducing pollution or preventing industries from releasing harmful substances into air, water, rivers, or groundwater. 

### **Shared Responsibility**

* Health promotion fosters shared responsibility.
* It shifts the locus of health initiatives and control over determinants from medical institutions and health professionals to individuals, families, schools, and worksites. 
* Greater collaboration among sectors, organizations, and individuals is necessary. 

### **Participation**

* Participatory strategies make programs more relevant and appropriate for the people affected. 
* People become more committed to changes they helped design or adapt for their own circumstances. 
* The transcript stresses that participation should include all stakeholders, not only beneficiaries. 

### **Ecological Approaches**

* Ecological approaches view health as the result of interdependence between the individual and the ecosystem. 
* The ecosystem includes family, community, culture, and the physical and social environment. 
* Health promotion should apply integrated strategies appropriate to the local context. 
* The transcript adds that local stakeholders must be engaged so that strategies fit the actual setting. 

## **Key Value Driving Health Promotion**

* The fundamental value behind health promotion is that the highest attainable standard of health is a fundamental right of every human being. 
* This right applies without distinction of race, religion, political belief, economic condition, or social condition. 
* The lecture uses this principle to support equity and empowerment in health promotion. 

## **Lecture Summary**

* Health is best understood through the WHO definition because it is holistic, even though it is harder to measure than the biomedical definition. 
* Health is shaped by multiple determinants, including social, economic, physical, and individual factors. 
* Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over and improve their health, and it goes beyond individual behavior to include social and environmental action. 
* It operates through health education and health protection. 
* Its scope includes empowerment, policy and program interventions, shared responsibility, participation, and ecological approaches. 
* The final aim is improved health outcomes, reduced inequities, and better quality of life, while treating health as a fundamental human right.
* The transcript closes by restating that the lecture is only the first step before moving into deeper concepts of health promotion and later into research methods in the subject.

## **Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion**

* The Ottawa Charter came from the First International Conference on Health Promotion, held in Ottawa on 21 November 1986.
* It built on earlier progress made through the Declaration on Primary Health Care at Alma-Ata, the WHO’s Targets for Health for All document, and intersectoral action for health.
* The Ottawa Charter remains a vital reference for health promotion.
* The transcript describes the Ottawa Charter as the starting point and basic reference point for health promotion, and says it helped establish health promotion as a discipline.

## **Health Promotion Logo**

* The logo represents a circle with three wings.
* It incorporates:

  * **Five key action areas in health promotion**

    * Build healthy public policy
    * Create supportive environments for health
    * Strengthen community action for health
    * Develop personal skills
    * Reorient health services
  * **Three basic strategies in health promotion**

    * Enable
    * Mediate
    * Advocate

### **Main Graphic Elements of the Logo**

* The red outside circle represents **building healthy public policies** and symbolizes that healthy public policies hold all five key action areas of health promotion.
* The round spot inside the circle represents the three basic strategies of health promotion: **enabling, mediating, and advocacy**, which are applied to all action areas.
* The three wings represent the key action areas for health promotion.
* The upper wing represents:

  * Strengthening community action
  * Developing personal skills
  * The need for the policy circle to constantly react to changes in society, communities, and individuals.
* The middle wing on the right side represents creating supportive environments for health.
* The bottom wing represents reorienting health services toward preventing diseases and promoting health.
* The transcript explains that the central strategies apply to all action areas, and that any health promotion intervention or research strategy will ultimately fall into one or more of these strategies.

## **Priorities for Health Promotion in the 21st Century**

* The Jakarta Declaration emphasized combining strategies, taking a comprehensive approach, and recognizing the necessity of participation.
* The key priorities identified were:

  * Promoting social responsibility for health
  * Increasing investments for health development
  * Consolidating and expanding partnerships for health
  * Increasing community capacity and empowerment
  * Securing an infrastructure for health promotion
* The transcript explains that the Jakarta Declaration became the major thrust for health promotion in the 21st century after the Ottawa Charter.

## **Key Principles Guiding Health Promotion**

### **Context**

* Health promotion focuses on population health and its context, especially the underlying social and economic determinants.
* Context is used for analyzing gaps in health and disease patterns in populations.

### **Integration**

* Promoting health means addressing the multidimensional nature of health.
* The lecture mentions the physical, social, and mental dimensions, and often spiritual health as well.
* Integration means combining the major dimensions of health as stated in the WHO definition.

### **Overall Responsibility of the State**

* All levels of government have responsibility and accountability for protecting, maintaining, and improving the health of citizens.
* Health must be included as a major component in government action.

### **Good Health as a Public Good**

* Good health benefits society as a whole.
* It supports both social development and economic development.

### **Participation**

* Participation of people and their communities in improving and controlling the conditions for health is a core principle in health promotion.
* Community participation means active involvement in planning, implementing, and monitoring a program aimed at well-being.
* Community participation is not passive use of services.
* Individuals and communities need to participate from the definition of the problem through to the generation of adequate solutions.
* The level of engagement may vary depending on interest and capabilities.
* The lecture states clearly that participation is the core principle in health promotion research and actions.

## **Lecture Takeaway**

* Ottawa Charter is a landmark in health promotion.
* The health promotion logo signifies five action areas and three strategies.
* The key guiding principles include context, integration, state responsibility, public good, and stakeholder participation.
* Participation is the core principle in health promotion research and actions.

# The Health Promotion Research Process
## Lecture 1/3

**Lecturer Details**

* Dr. Arista Lahiri, MD, MBA
* Assistant Professor (Community Medicine)
* Dr. B. C. Roy Multi-Speciality Medical Research Centre, Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur
* Email: [alahiri@bcrmrc.iitkgp.ac.in](mailto:alahiri@bcrmrc.iitkgp.ac.in) 

**Lecture Focus**

* Overview of health promotion research
* Steps in health promotion research process 

## **Overview of Health Promotion Research**

* Health promotion research is the process of discovering new ideas that can ultimately improve the health and well-being of the public. 
* It is an iterative process, meaning that when one research question is answered, several new questions usually emerge. 
* Health promotion research is also a public venture, not just a technical exercise. 
* Conclusions from this kind of research often have a direct impact on public health. 
* The lecture gives the example of vaccine acceptance studies, including COVID-19 and HPV, where research on individual and social determinants helped public health efforts reduce barriers such as cost and inconvenience. 
* The transcript reinforces that health promotion research differs from disease-oriented research because it is built around prevention, public health, and public good. 

## **Steps in the Health Promotion Research Process**

* The research process includes nine linked steps:

  * Defining the research population
  * Defining the research goal and specifying the exact research questions
  * Determining whether the research should be observational or experimental
  * Selecting a research design that provides a rigorous test of the research question
  * Determining the variables that must be measured
  * Selecting the sampling procedure
  * Implementing the research plan
  * Analyzing the data
  * Disseminating the findings 
* The transcript groups these into decision points, planning steps, and action points, showing that the process is systematic and iterative. 

## **Step One: Defining the Research Population**

* Since elimination of health disparities is a priority, health promotion research usually seeks solutions to problems that disproportionately exist within a defined population. 
* The researcher must specify the parameters that describe the target population. 
* The population is not chosen arbitrarily; it is based on known epidemiology. 
* The transcript explains that problems begin with people and populations, so identifying the right population comes before refining the research question. 

## **Step Two: Defining the Research Goal and Specifying the Exact Research Questions**

* Narrow and precisely defined goals and questions are better suited for rigorous research than broad ones. 
* Reviewing recent and relevant empirical literature helps prevent overly broad goals. 
* The research goal is a general statement of the purpose of the study. 
* Because the goal lacks precision, the researcher must move on to specific research questions. 
* Specific objectives can then be developed from these research questions. 
* The lecture example asks about the impact of an intervention on adolescent tobacco use when convenience store owners in high-density tobacco-sale areas improve compliance. 
* The transcript stresses that literature review, including even media reports or grey literature, helps identify a specific and answerable research question. 

## **Step Three: Determining Whether the Research Should Be Observational or Experimental**

* Observational research asks why people do what they do. 
* It does not involve treatment or intervention and is generally less time-consuming. 
* Experimental research involves manipulating a variable, which may include education, policy change, or environmental change. 
* Experimental research builds on observational research by asking how people can be helped to achieve positive change. 
* The lecture emphasizes that developing effective interventions takes time and effort, so researchers should proceed carefully and slowly. 
* The transcript gives the practical contrast: observational studies look at existing patterns, while experimental studies try to change barriers or behavior. 

## **Step Four: Selecting a Research Design That Provides a Rigorous Test**

* Research design should be chosen by considering all possible factors, including the outcome and available resources. 
* The guiding principle is parsimony. 
* Parsimony means the research design should do the job well without going beyond what is necessary. 
* The transcript explains this through resource hierarchy: qualitative research generally needs fewer resources, cross-sectional or trend studies need more, and experimental studies require the most. 

## **Step Five: Determining the Variables That Must Be Measured**

* A variable is anything that changes and must therefore assume a range of values. 
* The immediate goal is to identify every variable needed for a rigorous study. 
* The research question and literature review inform variable selection. 
* The variables selected are the ones measured and later analyzed. 
* Variables should be chosen carefully because once the study has started, there is no scope to go back and choose another group. 
* The transcript adds that the intervention itself can be treated as a variable because its status varies between groups. 

## **Step Six: Selecting the Sampling Procedure**

* The sampling procedure is one of the most important determinants of external validity. 
* External validity means the ability to generalize study findings to a population with similar characteristics. 
* Not every study needs a sampling procedure that produces high external validity. 
* Studies should still have a sufficient sample size to provide adequate and acceptable power for inference. 
* The transcript notes that some research, especially qualitative work, may use more flexible sampling depending on the objective. 

## **Step Seven: Implementing the Research Plan**

* Internal validity means consistency in implementing all study protocols and avoiding confounding caused by design, measurement, or poor implementation. 
* The research protocol should clearly specify procedures such as sampling, assignment to intervention conditions, timing and place of assessments, who conducts assessments, what participants are told, and how participants are encouraged to return for follow-up. 
* Drift occurs when research staff make subtle, unauthorized changes to intervention procedures. 
* The transcript emphasizes that strict consistency is necessary to preserve internal validity, unless the protocol itself allows a planned adaptive change. 

## **Step Eight: Analyzing the Data**

* After all assessments are completed and documented, a dataset can be established. 
* The dataset consists of the variables measured for each participant. 
* After checking for logical inconsistencies, the data are cleaned and the research process becomes dependent on statistical skills. 
* Parsimony remains important here because the goal is a rigorous and fair test of the research question without artificial procedures. 
* The transcript highlights that analysis must follow the protocol rather than using random tests from a statistical “battery.” 

## **Step Nine: Disseminating the Findings**

* Dissemination is a key part of the scientific process. 
* The study should have a high degree of rigor and be documented in the methodology. 
* The starting point of dissemination is transforming analytic results into carefully articulated findings. 
* Findings are the answers to the research questions generated by the data analysis. 
* Findings must be interpreted in the context of related research, showing how they strengthen or extend previous work. 
* Null findings and nonsignificant analyses can be just as important as significant findings in building the research base. 
* Findings may raise additional questions, which brings the research process back to its origin. 
* The transcript reinforces that dissemination is as important as every other step because it completes the iterative cycle and can generate new research questions. 

## **Core Takeaways**

* Health promotion research is a rigorous, systematic, and iterative process. 
* Its steps are connected, not isolated. 
* Dissemination matters as much as planning and implementation. 
* The final findings should answer the original research question and also open the door to further research. 

## **Resources Mentioned**

* Salazar LF, Crosby RA, DiClemente RJ. *Research Methods in Health Promotion*. 
* Saha I, Paul B. *Essentials of Biostatistics & Research Methodology*. 
* Dobe M. *Health promotion and Education: Foundations for Changing Health Behavior*.

# Process of Health Promotion Research: Integrity and Rigor
## Lecture-1/4
**Lecturer Details**

* Dr. Arista Lahiri, MD, MBA
* Assistant Professor (Community Medicine)
* Dr. B. C. Roy Multi-Speciality Medical Research Centre, Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur
* Email: [alahiri@bcrmrc.iitkgp.ac.in](mailto:alahiri@bcrmrc.iitkgp.ac.in) 

**Lecture Focus**

* Values in health promotion research
* Scholarship, grantsmanship, and ethics
* Integrity and rigor in research 

## **High Value Principles in Health Promotion Research**

* The lecture emphasizes that health promotion research demands vigilant attention to:

  * **Integrity** — best demonstrated through scholarships
  * **Rigor** — best observed in grantsmanship
  * **Ethics** — shown through approval of the empowered Ethics Committee before the research begins 
* These three values are presented as the core principles that guide meaningful health promotion research. 

## **Scholarship and Integrity**

* Scholarship means the researcher has an inherent curiosity about the research questions and a strong dedication to expanding the knowledge base in health promotion. 
* Integrity is a key feature of scholarship. 
* Integrity ensures a fair test of the research questions. 
* Integrity means that a preconceived desire to prove or disprove the hypothesis must not interfere with the research process. 
* The lecture points out that research itself forces objectivity, but adherence to the process depends on the researcher’s self-report, which is why integrity is vital. 

### **How Scholarship Reflects Integrity**

* Scholarship is not just interest in research for its own sake.
* It should show discipline-specific curiosity and a willingness to move the field of health promotion forward.
* In health promotion, this means curiosity should be linked to improving people’s lives and preventing disease and illness.
* A scholarship application is expected to reflect this kind of purpose and commitment. 

### **Why Integrity Matters**

* If a researcher has already secured prior scholarships, that can strengthen the appearance of integrity in later applications.
* Funding bodies often view prior success as evidence that the researcher has the integrity needed for the discipline.
* Even then, the researcher must still explain how the work will benefit people and why they are the right candidate for support. 

### **Integrity in Practice**

* Integrity means not allowing personal desire to interfere with reporting the truth.
* The lecture warns against false reporting.
* Example:

  * If an intervention was meant for 100 people but was actually given to only 50, reporting that it was given to 100 would be false reporting.
  * This distorts the evidence, misleads future research, and wastes resources. 

## **Grantsmanship and Rigor**

* Grantsmanship is a vital part of the research process. 
* Funding is not an explicit step in the research process, but it is an implicit demand that makes the entire research enterprise possible. 
* Executing rigor is expensive and resource-driven. 
* Obtaining funds for health promotion research is highly competitive. 
* Grant awards depend on several factors, including:

  * Quality of the research proposal
  * Relevance of the topic and population
  * The current degree of engagement in the iterative research process
  * The status of the idea or concept being pursued 

### **How Grantsmanship Relates to Rigor**

* Rigor requires proper resources.
* To follow a protocol line by line and make every measurement accurate, the researcher needs trained manpower, equipped devices, and other resources.
* Therefore, grantsmanship is closely linked with rigor because grants pay for the costs of doing research properly. 

### **Why Health Promotion Funding Is Competitive**

* Health promotion research is often preventive rather than curative.
* Because many funding systems give more attention to curative work, preventive and health promotion projects may receive a smaller share of available resources.
* That makes competitive grant writing especially important in this field. 

## **Attention to Ethics**

* Vigilant attention to ethics is the most critical issue in health promotion. 
* The lecture compares this to the Hippocratic Oath and states that health promotion researchers must follow the principle: **First, do no harm**. 
* Health promotion research is highly regulated by local and federal organizations that protect the rights of participants, including gatekeepers in some cases. 
* Health promotion research usually involves humans and often deals with very personal and protected behaviors. 

### **Why Ethics Needs Special Care**

* Health promotion aims to do good for the community.
* The intervention should not harm people, worsen disease risk, or create disability.
* Ethical review before the study begins is essential, but ethical responsibility continues during fieldwork as well. 

### **Ethical Complexity in Health Promotion**

* The lecture notes that an intervention may improve health but still create other harms.
* Example:

  * A health promotion intervention may improve one outcome but harm a person’s livelihood.
* This is why health promotion ethics are not always simple yes-or-no decisions.
* The researcher must think carefully about whether the intervention is appropriate for the study population. 

## **How the Three Values Fit Together**

* Scholarships showcase integrity. 
* Research grants pay for the expenses needed to achieve rigor. 
* Ethics is the most important of the three values in the health promotion research process. 
* Competence and proficiency in scholarship, grantsmanship, and ethics should be a high priority for meaningful health promotion research. 

## **Lecture Summary**

* **Scholarship** reflects curiosity, commitment, and integrity.
* **Grantsmanship** supports the resources needed to carry out rigorous research.
* **Ethics** protects participants and ensures that the study does no harm.
* Together, these three values form the foundation for meaningful health promotion research.

## **Resources Mentioned**

* Salazar LF, Crosby RA, DiClemente RJ. *Research methods in health promotion*. 
* Saha I, Paul B. *Essentials of Biostatistics & Research Methodology*. 
* Guttman, N. (2017), *Ethical issues in health promotion and communication interventions*.

## Lecture-1/5
**Lecturer Details**

**Lecture Focus**

* Basic principles
* Informed consent process
* Ethical concerns regarding health promotion communication interventions 

## **Ethics in Health Promotion Research: Overview**

* Health promotion research commonly involves biomedical or behavioral research. 
* Health promotion practice involves implementing biomedical or behavioral interventions. 
* Protection of human subjects and program participants is essential to minimize risk and maximize benefit in both research and practice. 
* Ethical conduct should be fundamental to any type of research, especially research involving human subjects. 
* Health promotion research often studies behaviors that are sensitive, such as mental health issues, sexual behavior, and substance use. 
* Health promotion communication interventions raise ethical issues because they aim to influence people’s views and lifestyles and are often shaped by government agencies or powerful public/private organizations. 

## **Basic Principles of Medical Research Ethics**

### **Autonomy**

* Autonomy means respect for individual rights.
* It is the right of an individual to decide whether to participate in research or not. 
* A person cannot be forced to participate in any study, whether intervention-based or non-interventional. 

### **Beneficence**

* The researcher should act in the best interest of the research participant. 
* The aim is to bring about more beneficial consequences than harmful ones. 

### **Non-maleficence**

* Non-maleficence means first do no harm. 
* The research procedure must not harm the participant. 
* The lecture stresses that even innovative or important research must still avoid harming participants. 

### **Justice**

* Justice means equal justice for all. 
* All individuals should have equal opportunity to participate in research. 
* No one should be unfairly compelled to participate, especially vulnerable populations. 
* The lecture points out that vulnerable groups such as prisoners, commercial sex workers, institutionalized children, payment dwellers, and similar groups must not be forced into studies. 

## **Informed Consent Process**

* Informed consent is a process in which the research participant has full access to rightful information related to the research, understands it fully, and signs agreement voluntarily. 
* The informed consent document has two parts:

  * Patient/participant information sheet
  * Informed consent form 
* The document should be mandatorily translated into the local language. 
* The informed consent document should be reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee before being administered for signature. 
* Assent refers to agreement provided by persons who are not of legal age to participate in research. 
* The lecture stresses that the participant must know the purpose of the study, why the researcher is approaching them, and what the possible consequences of participation are. 
* Privacy and confidentiality must be clearly promised to participants as part of the consent process. 

## **Ethical Concerns Regarding Health Promotion Communication Interventions**

### **Privacy, Autonomy, and Freedom of Choice**

* A key ethical concern is infringing on people’s privacy. 
* Another concern is interfering with the right to freedom of choice and autonomy in the name of promoting health. 
* Ethical issues can appear at every stage of the intervention:

  * conception of goals
  * informed consent
  * participation of the intended population in design
  * assessment of outcomes 
* Strategic decisions about which population should or should not be targeted are also ethical concerns. 
* The kind of persuasive arguments and visual appeals used in the intervention also matter ethically. 

### **Designing Interventions for Benefit**

* Health promotion interventions should respect people’s privacy and their autonomy to make free choices without manipulation. 
* They should be designed and implemented so they benefit most people and use public resources efficiently and effectively. 

### **Relevance of Solutions**

* Communication interventions that do not provide relevant solutions for the specific problems or barriers preventing recommended health practices raise a central ethical concern. 
* If people are not given solutions to the barriers they must overcome, they are less likely to adopt the recommended action or protect themselves. 
* The lecture gives the example of children being advised to do physical activity but not having a playground or proper space; in such a case, the intervention must also address the barrier. 

### **Digital Media and the Digital Divide**

* Digital media can widely disseminate health promotion information in many formats and are now a major source of health information for many people. 
* People with limited digital literacy, no physical access to computing facilities, or inadequate skills and competencies are less able to access or use online health information. 
* Public health emergencies that depend mainly on digital media can create an information gap. 
* Vulnerable groups such as the aged, the homeless, recent immigrants, rural residents, and poor populations are more likely to be at risk when digital media is the main route for information provision. 
* Digital interventions therefore raise ethical issues related to the digital divide and privacy. 

## **Key Takeaways**

* Ethical issues must always be the first concern in health promotion research and practice. 
* Digital media interventions need special attention because of privacy issues and unequal access. 
* Ethical issues should be identified especially in goals that appear most important or in strategies that seem most effective. 
* The lecture closes by emphasizing that ethics is not separate from health promotion work; it must be built into every stage of research and intervention. 

## **Resources Mentioned**

* Guttman, N. (2017), *Ethical issues in health promotion and communication interventions*. 
* Salazar LF, Crosby RA, DiClemente RJ. *Research methods in health promotion*. 
* Saha I, Paul B. *Essentials of Biostatistics & Research Methodology*.

# Health Behavior and Health Behavior Change

## **Health Behaviors**

* Positive, informed changes in health behaviors are the ultimate aim of health behavior change programs.
* In the broadest sense, health behavior includes the actions of individuals, groups, and organizations, along with the determinants, correlates, and consequences of those actions.
* Health behavior is not limited to personal action alone; it also includes social change, policy development and implementation, improved coping skills, and enhanced quality of life.

### **Definitions of Health Behavior**

* One definition describes health behavior as any activity undertaken by a person who believes himself or herself to be healthy, for the purpose of preventing disease or detecting it at an asymptomatic stage.
* Another definition describes health behavior as any activity undertaken to prevent or detect diseases or to improve health and well-being.

### **Types of Behaviors That Influence Health**

* Health-enhancing behaviors include exercise participation and healthy eating.
* Health-protective behaviors include vaccination against disease and condom use in response to the threat of HIV/AIDS.
* Health-detection behaviors include health screening, clinic attendance, and breast self-examination.
* Health-harming behaviors include smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, and health promotion also involves avoiding such behaviors.

### **Kasl and Cobb’s Three Categories of Health Behavior**

* **Preventive health behavior**

  * Any activity undertaken by a person who believes himself or herself to be healthy.
  * The purpose is to prevent disease or detect illness in an asymptomatic stage.
* **Illness behavior**

  * Any activity undertaken by a person who perceives himself or herself to be ill.
  * The purpose is to define the state of health and discover a suitable remedy.
* **Sick-role behavior**

  * Any activity undertaken by a person who considers himself or herself to be ill, for the purpose of getting well.
  * It includes receiving treatment from medical providers, usually involves dependent behaviors, and often leads to exemption from usual responsibilities.

## **Disciplinary Influences on Health Behavior Change over Time**

* Health behavior change has been addressed by multiple fields and professions, including health education, public health, psychology, social work, and various health and medical specialties.
* Clinical psychologists have traditionally focused on changing individuals.
* Social work tends to address individuals within their social and family contexts.
* During the 1970s and 1980s, health education emphasized individual behavior as a determinant of health status, which reduced attention to broader social determinants of health.
* Later, experts recommended that interventions on social and behavioral factors should link multiple levels of influence:

  * individual
  * interpersonal
  * institutional
  * community
  * policy levels

## **Settings for Health Behavior Change**

### **School**

* School-based health behavior change programs include classroom teaching, teacher training, and changes in school environments that support healthy behaviors.

### **Communities**

* Community-based health promotion draws on social relationships and organizations to reach large populations through media and interpersonal strategies.
* Community engagement and community mobilization help planners gain support and design suitable health messages and delivery mechanisms.
* Community participation is important because planners must understand community views, barriers, beliefs, and feasibility before designing interventions. The lecture stresses that community members must be included rather than having interventions imposed on them.

### **Workplace**

* The workplace is a source of both stress and social support because people spend so much time at work.
* Effective worksite programs can use social support as a buffer against stress and aim to improve worker health and health practices.

### **Health Care Settings**

* Health behavior change programs in health care settings include work with high-risk individuals, patients, their families, and the surrounding community.
* In-service training for health care providers is also part of health care-based health promotion.
* Primary care settings are especially important because they can reach a substantial number of people and improve population health.

### **Home**

* Health behavior change interventions can be delivered in homes through traditional public health approaches such as home visits.
* They can also be delivered through communication channels and media such as the Internet, telephone calls, and mail, depending on feasibility.

### **Marketplace**

* Marketplaces are important settings because they offer consumers fresh produce and other foods directly from producers, as well as ready-to-eat foods prepared by vendors.
* The lecture highlights the marketplace as a place where food hygiene, contamination risk, vendor behavior, and food handling practices can all become targets for health behavior change.

## **Audiences for Health Behavior Change**

### **Understanding the Target Audience**

* For interventions to be effective, strategies must be designed with an understanding of the recipients or target audiences.
* Important audience characteristics include health status, cultural context, social characteristics, beliefs, attitudes, values, skills, and past behaviors.
* Audiences may be reached as individuals, groups, through organizations, as communities, or through combinations of these approaches.
* Audiences may include health professionals, people at risk for disease, or patients.

### **Sociodemographic Characteristics and Ethnic/Racial Background**

* Audience characteristics such as gender, age, race, marital status, place of residence, and employment shape health behavior change efforts.
* Recognition of differences in disease and mortality across socioeconomic and ethnic or racial groups has increased efforts to reduce or eliminate health disparities.

### **Lifecycle Stage**

* Health education should be provided across the life cycle, from childbirth education to self-care education and rehabilitation for the very old.
* The lecture emphasizes a life-cycle approach, meaning the audience and intervention must fit the person’s stage of life.

### **Disease and At-Risk Status**

* People diagnosed with life-threatening diseases often experience symptoms as well as distress related to prognosis and medical decision-making.
* Timing, channels, and audiences for patient education should therefore be carefully considered.
* Successful patient education depends on a sound understanding of the patient’s viewpoint.
* Models and theories of health behavior can help suggest strategies to prevent relapse and improve maintenance of recommended practices for high-risk individuals.

## **Lecture Summary**

* Health behavior includes actions that prevent disease, detect disease early, protect health, improve health and well-being, and support quality of life.
* Health behavior change has shifted from an individual-only focus toward multilevel influence, including interpersonal, institutional, community, and policy levels.
* Effective health behavior change requires suitable settings such as schools, communities, workplaces, health care systems, homes, and marketplaces.
* Interventions must be designed for the right audiences, with attention to culture, social context, demographics, life stage, disease status, and risk status.
* The lecture closes by reinforcing that the goal is not only to start health behavior change, but to maintain it and prevent relapse over time.
